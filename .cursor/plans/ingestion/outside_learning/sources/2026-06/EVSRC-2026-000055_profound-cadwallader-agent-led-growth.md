This one is very relevant, but mainly for OMNI’s external interface / agent-facing legibility / distribution layer, not core clinical substrate.

It answers a question we have not fully handled yet:

What happens when the “user” discovering, comparing, interpreting, and recommending OMNI/Bloom/care options is no longer a human clicking links, but an agent acting for a human?

That is a real §C / surface / trust-boundary problem.

Core takeaway

The strongest idea is:

The front door did not just change from Google to ChatGPT. The visitor changed from human to agent.

James Cadwallader argues that marketers should not think of this as “new SEO.” In his framing, agents are now crawling, comparing, synthesizing, and steering decisions on behalf of users. Content may increasingly be discovered and consumed by agents rather than humans.

That matters for OMNI because future patients, providers, partners, vendors, and even other software systems may approach OMNI through agents first.

OMNI translation
1. OMNI needs an “agent-facing legibility” layer.

This is the big architectural gem.

Humans need websites, portals, dashboards, texts, phone calls, and explanations.

Agents need something else:

structured product/service facts,
policies,
eligibility rules,
safety boundaries,
pricing/entitlement facts,
provider/practice details,
trust/provenance,
APIs/capabilities,
machine-readable constraints,
source-of-truth references.

So OMNI should eventually have human-facing surfaces and agent-facing surfaces.

Not because we want to “game AI search,” but because if agents are going to interpret OMNI, OMNI needs to make itself legible without letting agents invent the answer.

Doctrine line:

Agent-facing legibility becomes part of product surface design.

2. This reinforces “publish truth, don’t manipulate retrieval.”

Profound’s marketing world is full of incentives to influence what ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc. say. That is useful to understand, but dangerous for OMNI.

For care, OMNI should not play “agent SEO” games. It should publish structured, truthful, scoped, up-to-date information that agents can safely retrieve.

Examples:

“Here are the actual services Bloom offers.”
“Here are contraindications / eligibility constraints.”
“Here is what CoolPeel aftercare means.”
“Here is what a patient agent may ask, but not decide.”
“Here is what requires licensed clinician review.”
“Here is what is informational vs medical advice.”

OMNI’s version is not:

“How do we rank #1 in AI answers?”

It is:

How do we ensure agents retrieve accurate, authorized, current, bounded truth about us?

3. “Agents consume the long tail” supports reservoir design.

The transcript says agents use a much wider surface area of the internet than humans, and may draw from dozens of sources for a single answer.

This matters for OMNI’s Knowledge Reservoirs.

Future agents will not only retrieve the homepage. They may ingest:

old blog posts,
outdated instructions,
pricing pages,
social clips,
third-party reviews,
Reddit-like comments,
YouTube transcripts,
provider bios,
PDFs,
old promos,
archived policy docs.

That creates a stale-source risk.

OMNI needs a way to declare:

current source,
deprecated source,
historical-only source,
superseded policy,
not medical advice,
patient-specific review required,
official canonical page.

This maps perfectly to what we just built for source ingestion: retrieval finds candidates, not authority.

4. “Original insight” is the anti-slop lesson.

The transcript makes a strong point: if agents already know the internet, the only useful content is original insight — things the model could not already infer from generic web data.

For OMNI, this means the valuable external content is not generic “What is GLP-1?” material.

It is:

Bloom’s actual care philosophy,
real patient journey insights,
real before/after operational learnings,
provider-specific nuance,
service-specific protocols,
patient misunderstandings,
aftercare lessons,
longitudinal outcome insights,
local market trust signals,
workflow evidence.

In other words:

OMNI should publish differentiated truth from lived care operations, not generic medical slop.

5. “Dead internet” is a warning for Knowledge Reservoir freshness.

The video raises the possibility that if humans stop clicking websites, incentives to produce original web content weaken, and AI systems may consume/remix content without sustaining the original source ecosystem.

For OMNI, that means future external knowledge may become noisier, more synthetic, more circular, and harder to trust.

So OMNI’s reservoirs cannot assume “the internet” remains a clean source of truth. They need:

provenance,
source authority,
publication date,
freshness,
human-origin signal,
correction/retraction status,
canonical-home checks,
confidence labels.

This is especially important for clinical RAG.

6. Agentic advertising is a trust problem.

The transcript predicts conversational AI ads may become extremely effective because they can be personalized to user intent in the moment. It also raises the issue that users expect agents to be objective when making recommendations.

For OMNI, this is a major trust-boundary issue.

If patient agents, insurance agents, pharmacy agents, vendor agents, or marketing agents start influencing care choices, OMNI needs to know:

Is this recommendation paid?
Is this source sponsored?
Is this agent acting for the patient, a vendor, or a platform?
Is the information objective, promotional, or contaminated?
Does this affect consent or patient understanding?

Doctrine line:

Agent-mediated recommendations must preserve sponsorship, provenance, and conflict-of-interest signals.
