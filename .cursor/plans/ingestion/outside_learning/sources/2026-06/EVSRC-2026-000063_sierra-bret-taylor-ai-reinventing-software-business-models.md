This one is major for OMNI’s business model and product-surface thesis.

Not CNS substrate as directly as LangChain / Serval / Pace, but this is one of the cleanest sources so far for:

AI agents as the new customer-facing front door, and outcomes-based pricing as the new software business model.

Bret Taylor’s credibility context matters here: he is not a random AI commentator. The transcript frames him as a legendary engineer/operator, former Facebook CTO, Salesforce leader context, OpenAI chair, and now co-founder of Sierra building customer-facing AI agents. So this source should be marked high-authority operator / application-layer founder perspective, not generic VC commentary.

Core takeaway

Sierra’s thesis is:

Every company’s main digital interface becomes an AI agent.

Not the website.
Not the mobile app.
Not the support portal.
The agent becomes the branded digital front door.

Bret says Sierra helps companies build their primary customer-facing AI agent, and he expects that if customers encounter a branded AI agent “in the wild,” Sierra wants it powered by their platform. He also argues that applied AI will likely manifest as vertical/domain-specific agents that do valuable jobs, not generic horizontal tools.

For OMNI, that is both validating and dangerous.

OMNI translation
1. Bloom / OMNI likely needs a “front-door agent” — but not a god-agent.

This video strengthens the idea that patients, clients, partners, and maybe providers will increasingly expect to interact with an intelligent agent rather than a static website or portal.

For Bloom/OMNI, that could mean:

“Can I get CoolPeel?”
“What should I do before my appointment?”
“I’m having nausea after my medication.”
“Can I reschedule?”
“What does this lab mean?”
“Can my wife ask about my aftercare?”
“Do I need provider review?”
“What’s included in my membership?”

But the danger is calling that one agent and letting it blur everything.

Sierra can talk about “the singular agent” for a brand. OMNI needs a more careful version:

One coherent front door; many governed capabilities behind it.

The patient/client should experience one trusted Bloom/OMNI interface. But underneath, the system must route into Identity, Scheduling, Messaging, D6 commerce, D7 documents, Observation, Clinical Memory, CNS, provider review, consent, and human escalation.

Doctrine:

The front-door agent is a surface. It is not the authority layer.

2. “Brand agent” matters more in healthcare than in normal SaaS.

A branded agent is not just a support bot. It expresses:

tone,
trust,
boundaries,
what the company will and will not answer,
when humans step in,
how uncertainty is handled,
what gets escalated,
what is considered official.

In healthcare/medspa, this matters massively. A Bloom agent cannot feel like generic ChatGPT. It has to feel like Bloom while remaining medically cautious.

So OMNI should distinguish:

brand/personality layer: how Bloom speaks;
clinical authority layer: what Bloom is allowed to say/do;
domain truth layer: what is actually true;
action layer: what can be changed or committed.

Keeper:

Brand voice can shape the response; authority state must constrain the response.

3. Outcomes-based pricing is strategically huge, but care requires caution.

Bret says Sierra uses outcomes-based pricing: when the agent resolves a customer issue autonomously, Sierra charges a pre-negotiated rate; if it escalates to a person, that interaction is free. He frames this as a natural evolution from perpetual software licenses to SaaS subscriptions to “pay for a job well done.”

For OMNI, this is a big business-model clue.

OMNI may eventually price around outcomes/work, not seats:

completed intake review,
resolved scheduling issue,
completed follow-up,
provider-ready chart packet,
reconciled appointment/payment/document mismatch,
completed post-procedure check-in,
refill-readiness review prepared,
lab follow-up routed,
patient message safely triaged.

But caution: clinical/care “outcomes” are not the same as customer-support resolutions. Medical outcomes, billing incentives, patient safety, Stark/AKS-type issues, payer rules, and clinical liability make this more delicate.

So the OMNI-safe version is probably:

Price around completed governed work units and operational outcomes before pricing around clinical outcomes.

4. This reinforces D5 / actualized work and service occurrence thinking.

Outcome pricing only works if OMNI knows what work actually happened.

That connects directly to D5:

Was a service occurrence completed?
Was a work item actualized?
Was a follow-up completed?
Was an agent action approved?
Did a human intervene?
Was the patient issue resolved?
Was there a domain commit?
Was there evidence/proof?

If OMNI ever prices work/outcomes, it needs a strong actualized-work substrate. Otherwise outcome pricing becomes vibes.

Doctrine:

Outcome pricing requires outcome proof.

5. Vertical specialization beats horizontal platform fluff.

Bret is skeptical of pure horizontal AI platforms for enterprise use. His rule of thumb is: the closer you get to solving a real company problem, the more successful the business is likely to be. He strongly favors vertical/domain-specific AI because each industry’s actual job, constraints, and workflows differ.

This is exactly OMNI.

OMNI should not sell:

“AI platform for healthcare.”

It should solve concrete work:

“GLP-1 intake-to-follow-up operating system.”
“Medspa patient journey + provider task + aftercare coordination.”
“Async care review and patient communication substrate.”
“Appointment/payment/documentation reconciliation.”
“Care operations agent system for high-volume recurring workflows.”

The more specific the wedge, the more credible the product.

Keeper:

Horizontal substrate, vertical wedge.

6. “Speak the customer’s language” is not sales fluff — it is product architecture.

Bret says selling requires asking questions, listening, understanding the customer’s business, and reflecting value through the problems they described. He warns that AI vendors all sound the same.

For OMNI, this matters because clinics/providers/patients do not care about:

CNS,
D5,
Knowledge Reservoirs,
capability envelopes,
stochastic intelligence,
agent traces.

They care about:

“My team misses follow-ups.”
“Patients ask the same questions.”
“Providers are overloaded.”
“Labs don’t get reviewed cleanly.”
“Front desk gets buried.”
“No one knows what happened.”
“Patients fall through cracks.”
“Documentation is a mess.”
“We lose revenue when scheduling/payment/aftercare break.”

OMNI architecture can be deep, but the product language must be workflow-native.

7. “Don’t let what you’re good at become who you are” is personally relevant to OMNI.

This line is more founder/operator doctrine than system doctrine, but it matters.

You are very good at architecture synthesis. That cannot become OMNI’s identity at the expense of product proof.

Bret’s lesson from Sheryl Sandberg was: do the most important thing for the job, even if it is not the thing that feels most natural or delightful.

For OMNI, that means:

Architecture has to become wedge, workflow, proof, customer value, and revenue.

Not because architecture is bad. Because architecture is only justified if it makes the company better at doing the real work.

8. The “do not spend time on undifferentiated scaffolding” warning is directly relevant.

Bret says startups should not waste time on things that are not core; every ounce of attention spent on commodity scaffolding is attention not spent on differentiation.

This is a hard check on OMNI.

building every infrastructure component ourselves,

over-custom internal tools before product proof,

Vertically integrate where truth/authority/workflow moat requires it; outsource commodity scaffolding.
