This is very high-signal, but in a different lane than the agent videos.

This is not mainly about frontier AI architecture. It is about how a real enterprise workflow platform becomes huge, chooses products, integrates AI, protects margins, sells outcomes, and expands from a core buyer into adjacent buying centers. For OMNI, that is extremely relevant.

Core takeaway

The strongest idea is:

AI only matters when it improves a real use case for a real buyer with a measurable outcome.

CJ Desai keeps returning to brutal product discipline: Who is the buyer? Do we have access to that buyer? Is the buyer next door or five doors down? Who are we competing against? What is the size of the prize? Can this become a billion-dollar ARR product? ServiceNow’s growth was not “we built a platform and wandered.” It was platform + buyer access + precise use-case expansion + organic execution.

For OMNI, this is a major correction against architecture drift.

OMNI translation
1. ServiceNow validates OMNI as workflow substrate — but only if we stay buyer/use-case disciplined.

CJ describes ServiceNow as orchestrating workflows: tasks sequenced digitally between humans and machines. He gives the example of ordering a corporate Mac, which triggers approvals, inventory, imaging, security credentials, shipping, and delivery.

That is basically the enterprise cousin of OMNI.

OMNI’s version:

patient submits intake,
eligibility is checked,
payment/entitlement is verified,
provider reviews,
documentation is generated,
consent is captured,
message is sent,
follow-up is scheduled,
labs are requested,
care obligations are tracked.

So yes, ServiceNow validates the “workflow OS” instinct.

But the lesson is not “build a giant platform.” The lesson is:

Pick the painful workflow, know the buyer, prove the outcome, then expand.

2. “AI in service of use cases” is a keeper.

CJ says ServiceNow has been focused on AI in service of their use cases. That phrase is huge. He is not saying “add AI everywhere.” He is saying: if a customer already uses ServiceNow for a workflow, AI should accelerate that workflow or improve that outcome.

OMNI needs exactly this discipline.

Not:

“Where can OMNI use AI?”

But:

“Which care/business use case becomes meaningfully better because AI is inside the governed workflow?”

Examples:

intake-to-provider-review,
patient message triage,
post-procedure aftercare,
lab follow-up,
GLP-1 refill readiness,
appointment/payment/documentation reconciliation,
missing-info collection,
provider packet preparation.

Keeper:

AI is not the product. AI is leverage applied to a use case.

3. Smaller, use-case-specific models are strategically important.

CJ explicitly rejects the idea that ServiceNow can just run giant frontier models everywhere because gross margin matters. He frames it as a constraint-driven optimization problem: smaller models, lower latency, lower cost, specific ServiceNow use cases, runnable on available infrastructure.

This reinforces OMNI’s model-routing doctrine.

OMNI should not default to “giant model everywhere.” It needs:

small models for routine classification,
mid models for summarization/context packets,
frontier models for high-uncertainty reasoning,
deterministic validators for authority and commit,
human review for high-stakes action.

Keeper:

Model strategy is product economics.

4. Constraint-driven innovation is a real Build OS principle.

ServiceNow’s gross-margin constraint forced better AI architecture: smaller models, use-case-specific optimization, open-source leverage, and infrastructure discipline.

OMNI should embrace constraints instead of treating them as blockers.

Constraints:

clinical safety,
provider attention,
PHI,
consent,
cost,
latency,
staff adoption,
patient trust,
regulatory boundaries,
domain truth.

The right OMNI posture:

Constraints are not anti-innovation. They define the innovation path.

5. Outcome-based selling is non-negotiable.

CJ is blunt: customers do not care about another AI pitch. They care about ROI. He says if you are not doing outcome-based selling, you are just another person showing up with an AI pitch.

OMNI needs this badly.

Do not sell:

“AI care orchestration platform.”

Sell:

reduce missed follow-ups,
reduce provider admin time,
improve intake completion,
speed patient response,
reduce front desk workload,
prevent scheduling/payment/documentation mismatch,
increase provider-ready reviews,
reduce unresolved tasks,
improve adherence and trust.

Keeper:

OMNI must sell measured workflow outcomes, not AI sophistication.

6. “Core has to be core” is a hard warning.

CJ says ServiceNow did not really push Act Two until after the core was enormous. His advice is to deeply understand the TAM and core problem before scattering into multiproduct expansion.

This hits OMNI directly.

OMNI has many possible futures:

telehealth OS,
medspa OS,
clinical RAG,
agent workbench,
business ops,
patient journey,
provider tasking,
workforce/payroll,
robotics readiness,
external agent protocols.

All may matter later. But the wedge cannot be all of them.

Doctrine:

The core must become real before the platform expands.

7. Buyer access matters as much as product architecture.

CJ’s buyer discipline is brutal: who is the buyer, do we have access, will the current buyer introduce us, is the prize big enough?

For OMNI, this means we need a clear first buyer.

Possible buyers differ:

medspa owner/operator,
telehealth operator,
medical director,
provider group,
practice manager,
front desk/admin lead,
compliance/clinical ops,
enterprise CIO someday.

A brilliant substrate with no buyer path is not a company.

Keeper:

Architecture without buyer access is not strategy.

8. The CIO point maps to OMNI’s future enterprise buyer, but not necessarily the first wedge.

CJ argues the CIO is not irrelevant; in large enterprises, the CIO is still the technical executive asked to produce the AI roadmap and control cost/revenue paths.

For OMNI, long term, yes: CIO/CTO/ops leadership may matter in large health systems or multi-site operators.

But early OMNI likely should not start by selling CIO abstraction. It should start where the workflow pain is undeniable and close to the operator.

Short version:

Long-term enterprise buyer may be CIO/COO. Early wedge buyer is probably the operator who feels the workflow pain daily.

9. The “what worked / didn’t work” section is very important.

CJ says document understanding — invoices, AP, paperwork matching — remained hard despite many attempts. But prediction/pattern-matching around use cases worked better, like identifying an employee’s upcoming computer refresh entitlement and prompting a human-in-loop confirmation.

This is useful for OMNI.

AI may work best first where:

the pattern is known,
the workflow is existing,
the action is bounded,
the human can confirm,
the expected output is clear,
the system can predict next best action.

That sounds like:

follow-up due,
refill window approaching,
intake incomplete,
consent missing,
appointment change created payment/document issue,
provider review overdue,
patient likely needs clarification,
post-procedure check-in due.

Keeper:

Start with bounded prediction + human confirmation before trying to automate ambiguous document-heavy judgment.

ServiceNow threatens generic enterprise workflow platforms. But OMNI should not be generic enterprise workflow.

ServiceNow orchestrates enterprise work. OMNI must orchestrate governed care work.
