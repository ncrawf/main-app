This one is not core OMNI product doctrine, but it is very important infrastructure doctrine.

It is the reminder that AI is not floating magic. It is steel, power, cooling, land, supply chains, permitting, networking, labor, factories, and speed. For OMNI, the lesson is not “build data centers.” The lesson is:

Intelligence has physical substrate. Any serious AI company eventually has to understand the bottlenecks beneath the model.

Core takeaway

Chase Lochmiller’s central claim is that the AI data center is no longer just a building full of servers. It is an AI factory and, more sharply, the data center is becoming the new unit of compute. The constraints are no longer only chips or software; they are power, steel, switchgear, cooling, networking, skilled labor, and physical deployment speed. Crusoe’s example is extreme: thousands of workers on-site, gigawatts of capacity under construction, and even manufacturing switchgear themselves to compress lead times from roughly 100 weeks to 22 weeks.

OMNI translation
1. This is a warning against “AI abstraction blindness.”

OMNI can use OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, local models, cloud inference, and future agent platforms — but underneath all of them is a physical compute supply chain.

That matters because OMNI’s future architecture includes:

ambient agents,
long-horizon traces,
multimodal ingestion,
voice,
clinical photos,
wearable/sensor data,
build agents,
simulation,
deep reasoning,
retrieval,
evals,
patient-facing agents,
provider workbenches.

Those things may look like software features, but they carry compute, latency, cost, storage, and reliability consequences.

Keeper:

AI architecture must include runtime economics and substrate constraints, not just intelligence dreams.

2. “Data center as computer” maps to OMNI as “workflow system as computer.”

Chase’s point is that the unit of compute has shifted upward: not chip, not server, not rack — the whole data center becomes the compute system.

For OMNI, the analogy is useful:

The unit of care intelligence is not one model call. It is the whole governed workflow system.

A patient reply is not just a prompt. It may involve:

identity,
consent,
patient history,
clinical memory,
observations,
D7 documents,
scheduling,
commerce/entitlement,
provider availability,
CNS orchestration,
policy checks,
message rail,
audit,
follow-up.

So the care “computer” is the whole OMNI substrate.

Doctrine line:

The model call is not the product; the governed workflow system is the product.

3. Vertical integration is justified when the bottleneck is existential.

Crusoe did not vertically integrate switchgear because it was fun. They did it because 100-week lead times would destroy their ability to move.

That is exactly the test OMNI needs.

OMNI should not build everything. But it should own layers where outsourcing would destroy:

truth,
authority,
workflow coherence,
safety,
learning,
trust,
speed.

So yes, OMNI may outsource commodity scaffolding. But it probably cannot outsource:

clinical/domain authority logic,
care workflow orchestration,
context packet structure,
evidence/observation/adoption layering,
agent traces/evals,
patient/provider workbench experience,
Knowledge Reservoir promotion rules.

Keeper:

Vertically integrate only where the bottleneck controls truth, trust, speed, or safety.

4. Energy-first thinking maps to “constraint-first architecture.”

Crusoe’s Abilene strategy was energy-first: go where abundant clean energy and curtailment existed, instead of forcing old data-center geography.

OMNI needs the same mental model, but for care workflows.

Do not begin with:

“Where can we put AI?”

Begin with:

“Where is the real constraint?”

Examples:

provider attention,
staff follow-through,
missing patient info,
unreviewed labs,
unclear ownership,
scheduling/payment/documentation mismatch,
patient confusion,
clinical-risk escalation,
trust,
regulatory authority.

Then build around the constraint.

Doctrine line:

Design from the bottleneck backward.

5. Sovereign AI / data locality is a preview of healthcare federation.

Chase talks about sovereign AI and why countries want data/compute locally because certain data cannot leave a jurisdiction.

That maps hard to healthcare.

OMNI will eventually have similar locality/federation problems:

state-specific care rules,
operator-specific data custody,
practice-level ownership,
patient consent boundaries,
external partner access,
cross-clinic sharing,
vendor processing limits,
model-provider data controls,
region-specific compliance.

So this supports the Federation / Operator / Tenant doctrine:

AI capability may be global, but care data authority is local, scoped, and jurisdictional.

6. Physical infrastructure culture has a Build OS lesson.

Crusoe’s “think like a mountaineer” culture is relevant: plan A matters, but you must expect chaos and design resilience into processes and organization.

OMNI needs that posture.

Architecture cannot assume perfect workflows. Care is messy:

patient does not answer,
staff forgets,
provider disagrees,
lab is delayed,
consent is missing,
payment fails,
model output is uncertain,
agent gets stuck,
state changes mid-run.

OMNI should not merely optimize for the happy path.

Keeper:

Care-grade architecture needs plan B built in.

7. “Manufacturing intelligence” should be treated carefully.

Chase’s phrase is powerful: we can now manufacture intelligence with silicon.

For OMNI, that is directionally true, but the care translation must be restrained:

We can manufacture reasoning capacity. We cannot manufacture authority, trust, consent, or clinical responsibility the same way.

That is the boundary.

More compute can produce more intelligence.
It cannot automatically produce legitimacy.
