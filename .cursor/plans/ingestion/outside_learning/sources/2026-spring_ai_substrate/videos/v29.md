## 2. ChatGPT distillation

<Ingested. This one is not peripheral — this is a core missing OMNI primitive.

The key concept is:

Agentic consent is not static consent. It is delegated action authority with scope, lifetime, identity, context, and revocation.

The transcript defines agentic consent as answering: who delegated authority, what actions are permitted, and the scope/lifetime of that delegation. It also emphasizes that agents execute actions, not just generate output, so consent has to govern what they are allowed to do on someone’s behalf.

OMNI translation

This belongs in several places, not just “AI governance.”

1. Federation still matters — maybe more than before

Federation should own the topology of delegation:

who is acting
on whose behalf
for what purpose
against what data/resource/tool
under what relationship
with what consent
for how long
with what revocation path
with what audit trail

This is exactly the correction we already suspected: Federation is not partner portal plumbing. It is authority/provenance/delegation topology for humans, organizations, systems, and agents.

2. RBAC is not enough

Classic RBAC answers: “Does this actor have this permission?”

Agentic consent adds:

Is this action within the original delegated intent?
Has the context changed?
Is this still the same transaction?
Is this permission still fresh?
Does this require just-in-time confirmation?
Is the agent acting with the user/operator, or instead of them?

That means OMNI needs something like:

Delegated Authority Envelope

Not just role = provider or agent_can_read_messages = true.

3. Agent actions need transaction-scoped permission

The transcript stresses granular, time-restrained, transaction-based access. That is massive for OMNI.

For example:

“AI may summarize this patient’s chart for this provider right now.”
“AI may draft a message, but not send it.”
“AI may recommend a med adjustment, but not commit it.”
“AI may retrieve labs for this encounter, but not browse unrelated historical PHI.”
“AI may query commerce entitlement for eligibility, but not alter entitlement.”
“AI may prepare a refill task, but clinician must approve.”

This should become a first-class OMNI control pattern.

4. Just-in-time prompting = safety escalation primitive

The video’s “just-in-time prompting” idea maps cleanly to OMNI:

When policy is missing, scope changes, sensitivity increases, confidence drops, or action impact crosses a threshold, the agent must stop and ask for authorization.

In OMNI terms:

No-policy / changed-context / high-impact action → interrupt → human consent/approval → record decision → optionally seed future policy.

That is CNS-compatible.

5. Transparency, revocability, personalization

This is also Settings + patient/operator trust infrastructure.

OMNI eventually needs user-visible control over:

what agents can access
what agents can do
which consents are active
which permissions are recurring vs one-time
which automations are enabled
what was done on the user’s behalf
how to revoke or narrow authority

That is not “nice to have.” For a 1BN company in care, it is trust infrastructure.

Keeper doctrine

OMNI agents may not act merely because they are technically capable. They act only through a delegated authority envelope that binds identity, purpose, scope, resource, action, time, context, consent, policy, audit, and revocation.

And the sharper version:

AI autonomy in OMNI must be permissioned at the transaction level, not granted as a static global capability.

This one should definitely feed the AI-substrate pass, Federation native draft, RBAC, Settings, CNS actions, Messaging, D7 audit/materialization, and the Build OS agent-control model.>
