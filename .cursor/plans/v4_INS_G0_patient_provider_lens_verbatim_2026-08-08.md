A proposed rule was slated for March 2026 in the unified agenda (McDermott+), and as of June 2026 the AHA still describes AEOB as not implemented (AHA fact sheet, 2026‑06‑05). Good Faith Estimates exist only for the uninsured/self‑pay, and even there HHS exercises enforcement discretion on co‑provider and co‑facility charges (CMS GFE FAQ Part 3) — meaning the estimate you get is legally permitted to omit the anesthesiologist and the pathologist.
Step 4 — After care: the denial. In ACA marketplace plans in 2023, insurers denied 19–20% of in‑network claims and 36–37% of out‑of‑network claims, with in‑network denial rates ranging from 1% to 54% across issuers (KFF; the two KFF releases report 19% and 20% against slightly different claim universes, 425M vs 471M). The most common stated reason for denial was the literal category "other" at 34%; only 6% cited medical necessity and 9% cited prior authorization or referral. In Medicare Advantage, insurers made 52.8 million prior authorization determinations in 2024 and denied 4.1 million (7.7%) (KFF).
Step 5 — The EOB/bill confusion, and the wait. The patient receives an Explanation of Benefits that says "this is not a bill," then a provider statement that may or may not reconcile to it, sometimes months later, sometimes from three separate legal entities for one encounter. Fifty‑eight percent of insured adults report a problem using their insurance in the past 12 months; among people in fair or poor health it is 67%, and among heavy utilizers roughly three in four (KFF Survey of Consumer Experiences).
Step 6 — The time tax. More than half of respondents reported doing administrative tasks (scheduling, obtaining prior authorizations, resolving billing or premium problems) in the prior year; roughly one‑quarter reported delayed or foregone care because of an administrative task; among those with a billing problem or prior‑authorization requirement, a third delayed or missed care. One estimate puts US workers' time spent on health care administration at $21.6 billion a year (Kyle & Frakt, discussed in Milbank/PMC commentary).
Step 7 — The debt. Forty‑one percent of US adults report debt from medical or dental bills, broadly defined to include payment plans, credit cards, and loans from family (KFF). On the narrower, harder measure — unpaid bills over $250 in the 2021 SIPP — 20 million adults owe at least $220 billion; 14 million owe over $1,000; 3 million owe over $10,000 (Peterson‑KFF). Roughly one in five people with health care debt do not expect to ever pay it off, and about 20% of US hospitals will deny non‑emergency care to patients with an outstanding bill (KFF Health News, Diagnosis: Debt). That last fact is the one that closes the loop: unpaid debt becomes an access barrier, which is how a financing failure converts into a clinical one.
Step 8 — The clinical consequence. Fifty‑seven percent of underinsured adults avoided needed care because of cost, and 41% of adults who delayed care said a health problem worsened as a result (Commonwealth Fund 2024). The 2025 Affordability Survey (n=6,353, fielded July–October 2025, published June 2026) found that one in five privately insured working‑age adults experienced a coverage denial in the past year; among those with a prior‑authorization denial, 41% delayed care, 28% said a health problem worsened, and 63% reported worry or anxiety. Among those with a claim denial, nearly 70% said it cost them more money and 43% said they have medical debt they are still paying off (Commonwealth Fund).
Where it hurts, ranked by the patient's own testimony: (1) permission to get care at all (prior auth); (2) not knowing the price beforehand; (3) the retroactive bill that contradicts what you were told; (4) the debt and its downstream access and credit consequences; (5) the hours.
2. What patients want — said, chosen, and what would actually reduce harm
These three answers are genuinely different, and conflating them is how products die.
(a) What they say they want. Price before service. A single bill. Someone to explain what happened. A named human who is accountable. Not to fight. When KFF forced a ranking, they said: stop making me ask permission (34%), then help me understand what I owe (23%).
(b) What they behaviorally choose. They do not shop. This is the most robustly replicated finding in the price‑transparency literature and it should be treated as a hard constraint, not a marketing problem. New Hampshire's HealthCost site was used by about 1% of state residents over three years (Tu & Lauer / Health Affairs, PubMed). The Truven Treatment Cost Calculator: 11% of 70,408 families used it once, 1% used it three or more times (Sinaiko et al.). Aetna's Member Payment Estimator reached 1.6% of eligible subscribers in year one and 2.4% in year two (AJMC). CalPERS' Castlight deployment produced neither high usage nor lower spending (narrative policy review, PMC).
What they do choose: they defer (57% of the underinsured), they go where their doctor sent them, they pay the bill or ignore it, and — increasingly — they route around the system entirely. Thirty‑two percent used an AI chatbot for health information in the past year; among those, 19% cited not being able to afford a professional and 18% cited not having a doctor or an appointment (KFF poll, fielded Feb 24–Mar 2, 2026). And they buy cash: LillyDirect sells Zepbound at $299–$449/month against a ~$1,086 list (RX Index tracker).
Notice the shape of the revealed preference: patients will not do comparison work, but they will accept a fixed, posted, take‑it‑or‑leave‑it price if it removes the negotiation. Shopping is work. A price tag is not.
(c) What would actually reduce harm. Ranked by the evidence, not by what polls well:
1. Fewer permission gates. 80.7% of appealed MA prior‑auth denials are overturned. The denial was wrong four times in five, and only 11.5% of denials are ever appealed.
2. Binding prices, not estimates. The distinction between "estimate" and "commitment" is the entire ballgame; see §3.
3. Automatic financial assistance rather than application‑based. Nonprofit hospitals themselves estimated that roughly $2.7 billion of their reported bad debt came from patients who were likely charity‑care eligible but did not receive it (KFF). A 2015 study found only 44% of hospitals notified patients about financial assistance before attempting collection (CFPB).
4. Default appeals. The harm is concentrated in the 88.5% of MA denials nobody contests.
5. Coverage continuity. All of the above is dominated by whether the person has coverage at all in 2026.
The gap between (a) and (b) is why "consumer‑directed health care" has under‑delivered for two decades. The gap between (b) and (c) is where the actual product opportunity sits.
3. Cost predictability — what "I know what this will cost" really requires
To make a binding statement of a patient's out‑of‑pocket cost before service, you must simultaneously resolve eight things, and every one of them is owned by a different party:
1. Identity and coverage as of the date of service — not today's eligibility, the eligibility that will be true when care happens. Coverage churn makes these different.
2. Benefit design applied to this specific service — covered? excluded? tier? visit limit?
3. Accumulator state — deductible met to date, out‑of‑pocket maximum to date, including claims that have been incurred but not yet adjudicated. This is the killer: the accumulator is a moving, lagged number and no party holds a real‑time authoritative copy.
4. Network status of every rendering entity in the encounter — surgeon, facility, anesthesia, pathology, radiology, assistant, implant vendor. Network status is per‑entity, per‑plan, per‑product, and per‑date.
5. The negotiated rate for each line — often expressed as a percentage of an external fee schedule or an algorithm rather than a dollar amount, which is exactly why CMS had to replace the "estimated allowed amount" in hospital machine‑readable files with actual 10th/median/90th‑percentile allowed amounts computed from 835 remittance data, effective January 1, 2026, enforced April 1, 2026 (CMS CY2026 OPPS fact sheet).
6. The clinical scope, which is not knowable in advance. A diagnostic colonoscopy that becomes therapeutic changes the code, the coverage category, and the patient's cost share mid‑procedure.
7. Medical necessity adjudication — a post‑hoc judgment that can retroactively void the whole estimate.
8. Coordination of benefits, secondary coverage, and any surprise‑billing protection that caps the patient's exposure.
Why it has failed. Three reasons, in order of weight:
* No one is bound. An estimate creates no obligation. Under the current architecture the only party who can bind the patient's cost is the plan, and the plan is not required to. Until an estimate is a commitment with a remedy for breach, it is marketing.
* The data is federated across parties with asymmetric incentives. The provider knows the clinical intent; the plan knows the benefit and accumulator; neither knows the other's half. The AEOB was the mechanism designed to force that exchange, and it is the one NSA provision still unimplemented — five years and three administrations in. The AHA now proposes a "mock claim" workaround that would send good‑faith estimates through existing 837 claims infrastructure (AHA), which is a tacit admission that the greenfield data‑exchange approach failed on integration cost.
* Transparency was built for researchers, not patients. Machine‑readable files are a compliance artifact. They are useful to employers, journalists, and price‑benchmarking vendors. They do not answer "what will I pay," because they contain no accumulator, no benefit design, and no identity.
The one place predictability does work is where it is achieved by removing the payer from the transaction: DPC memberships, medspa menu pricing, LillyDirect's flat $299/$399/$449. The lesson is uncomfortable: predictability is currently a property of disintermediation, not of transparency regulation.
4. Comprehension — information versus understanding
There is now abundant information and almost no understanding. Sixty‑five percent of individuals accessed a patient portal in 2024, up from 57% in 2022, and 77% were offered access (ASTP/ONC HINTS data brief). But 59% of people have multiple portals and only 7% use anything that combines them. The information exists, fragmented across the exact seams the patient cannot cross.
The comprehension failure has four distinct layers, and they need different fixes:
* Vocabulary. Deductible, coinsurance, out‑of‑pocket maximum, allowed amount, adjustment, patient responsibility, EOB. These are terms of art from an insurance contract the patient never read and could not enforce.
* Causality. The patient cannot tell why the number is the number. KFF found the single largest category of stated denial reason was "other" (34%). A reason code that does not explain is worse than silence, because it forecloses the question.
* Actionability. Only about 40% of consumers believe they have a legal right to appeal to a government agency or independent medical expert (KFF, via MedCity). They have that right. They don't know it.
* Temporality. The patient cannot tell whether a document is a prediction, a status report, or a final determination. "This is not a bill" is the system telling the patient it knows this is confusing and declining to fix it.
What the patient needs to be told, in this order, versus what data exists:
The patient needs to be told	The data that exists
"You will pay $X, and that is final unless Y changes"	An estimate with disclaimers, or nothing
"Here is why, in one sentence, in causal terms"	A CARC/RARC adjustment code, or "other"
"This is / is not final. If not final, here is what happens next and when"	Documents that don't declare their own status
"You are eligible for assistance; we already applied it"	An application form the patient must find
"You can contest this. Here is the button. It takes 4 minutes"	A right most people don't know they have
"This is the same event you were treated for in March"	Three unlinked bills from three tax IDs
The gap is not a data gap. It is an obligation‑to‑explain gap plus a longitudinal‑identity gap: nobody is required to tell the patient the causal story, and nobody holds the thread that says these six documents are one episode of care.
5. Agency and redress — what a wronged patient can actually do
Five channels exist. They vary enormously in effectiveness, and their effectiveness is inversely correlated with how many people use them.
Internal appeal. Universal right for non‑grandfathered plans. In marketplace plans in 2023, consumers appealed fewer than 1% of the 73–86 million denied in‑network claims, and insurers upheld 56% of the appeals filed (KFF). In Medicare Advantage, 11.5% of denials were appealed in 2024 — but 80.7% of those appeals overturned the denial, a rate above 80% in every year from 2019 through 2024 (KFF). Read those two numbers together: appealing works and almost nobody does it. That is the single largest unexploited patient‑welfare arbitrage in US health care.
External review (IRO / IMR). Post‑internal‑appeal, binding on the insurer, decided in ≤45 days standard and ≤72 hours expedited, costing the consumer at most $25 and often nothing (HealthCare.gov). Marketplace enrollees filed roughly 5,000 external appeals in 2023 — about 3% of upheld internal appeals. Outcomes are strongly favorable and wildly variable by state: an aggregation of state public records puts overturn rates from 17.0% (West Virginia) to 80.0% (Connecticut, a figure that includes pre‑decision insurer concessions after advocate involvement); Pennsylvania's new program overturned ~48% of 1,353 referred cases; California reports ~73% of IMR cases end with the enrollee getting the service (Apellica External Review Index — an aggregator, cell‑level sourcing claimed but not independently verified here). KFF cites a 64% reversal rate on external appeal in Maryland (KFF). ProPublica calls external review "one of the industry's best‑kept secrets" (ProPublica).
No Surprises Act protections and IDR. The NSA works for patients and has become a war between payers and providers that the patient never sees. AHIP/BCBSA (industry‑funded, treat with appropriate discount) estimate the law prevents over one million surprise bills a month and prevented more than 10 million in the first nine months of 2023 (AHIP). Meanwhile the arbitration channel exploded: 4.8 million cumulative disputes through the end of 2025 against a federal projection of ~17,000 per year, with 1.4 million filed in H2 2025 alone (Georgetown CHIR); CMS reports 257,724 disputes closed in January 2026 alone with initiations and closures now roughly balanced (CMS IDR reports). IDR is not patient redress. It is a provider‑payer settlement rail. The patient's only stake is that they are out of it.
State complaints and regulators. Effective, jurisdictionally fragmented, and invisible to most patients. Self‑funded ERISA plans — most large‑employer coverage — sit outside state insurance regulation entirely, which means the redress channel a patient is told about may not apply to them.
Credit reporting — be careful here, this changed twice. The CFPB's January 2025 rule (90 Fed. Reg. 3276) would have barred medical debt from credit reports entirely. On July 11, 2025, the US District Court for the Eastern District of Texas vacated it on a joint consent motion by the Bureau's new leadership and the industry plaintiffs in Cornerstone Credit Union League v. CFPB, holding the rule exceeded CFPB authority under FCRA and violated the APA — and, significantly, that FCRA expressly preempts state laws restricting medical‑debt reporting (CFPB's own page, page last modified 2026‑02‑24; Reuters; court opinion). What survives:
* The voluntary 2022 bureau policies (paid medical collections removed; unpaid under $500 removed; 12‑month waiting period), which CRS estimates eliminated roughly 70% of medical tradelines — and which the bureaus may reverse at any time (CRS IF12169).
* State statutes. CRS counted 11 states as of June 2025; secondary trackers count 15 in 2026 (MultiState; a consumer‑facing tracker lists CA, CO, CT, DE, IL, ME, MD, MN, NJ, NY, OR, RI, VT, VA, WA). Their status is genuinely unresolved: the Cornerstone court's preemption reasoning is not binding outside E.D. Tex., no court has struck a state statute on that basis, and secondary sources report an October 2025 CFPB interpretive rule asserting FCRA preemption (NOT VERIFIED against a CFPB primary document in this pass).
* The scale of what was lost: CFPB estimated the vacated rule would have removed roughly $49 billion in reported debt for about 15 million people and raised affected scores an average of ~20 points (MultiState).
The honest summary of patient agency: the tools that work best (external review, appeals) are the ones patients least know exist; the tool with the most volume (IDR) isn't theirs; and the protection with the broadest reach (credit reporting) was federally granted and federally withdrawn inside seven months, leaving a state‑by‑state patchwork of contested validity.
6. Portability from the patient's view
Patients change jobs, plans, doctors, and cities. Here is what survives the transition and what does not.
What the patient assumes follows them	What actually follows	What breaks
Their medical record	A retrievable copy, if they know to request it, per portal, per system	Continuity of interpretation — the new clinician gets documents, not the reasoning
Their deductible progress	Nothing	Accumulators reset at plan change; mid‑year job changes can mean paying two deductibles in one year
Their prior authorization	Nothing	An approved course of therapy must be re‑approved by the new plan, mid‑treatment
Their formulary position / step‑therapy history	Nothing	The patient re‑fails drugs they already failed
Their doctor	Only if the doctor is in the new network at the new plan	Established relationship terminated by a contracting decision the patient never saw
Their care team's shared context	Nothing	Every referral restarts the story
Their financial‑assistance determination	Nothing, and it is per‑hospital anyway	Re‑application, or more likely no application
Their debt	Yes. Debt is the most portable object in the system.	—
That last row is the whole indictment. The only thing with reliable longitudinal identity across payers, providers, employers, and geographies is the obligation to pay. Clinical history, authorization history, accumulator state, and eligibility determinations all die at the boundary; the receivable follows you across state lines for seven years.
There is a partial federal remedy in flight. CMS‑0057‑F requires impacted payers (MA, Medicaid/CHIP FFS and managed care, FFE QHPs) to stand up a Payer‑to‑Payer FHIR API that moves claims, encounter, clinical, and prior‑authorization data when a patient changes plans, plus a Patient Access API that includes prior authorizations, by January 1, 2027; the operational prior‑authorization rules (7 calendar days standard, 72 hours expedited, specific denial reasons, public metric reporting) took effect January 1, 2026 (CMS fact sheet). Two caveats. It requires the patient's permission and therefore the patient's awareness. And it does not cover ERISA self‑funded employer plans, which is where most working‑age Americans actually are.
What patients wish followed them, in their own framing: "the fact that this was already approved," "the fact that I already paid my deductible," "the fact that this drug didn't work," and "the person who knows my history."
7. Trust
Trust in US health care in 2026 is bifurcated by function: high for clinical judgment, low for financial and permission decisions, and increasingly redirected toward tools nobody in the industry controls.
* Clinicians retain trust. Eighty‑five to 88% trust health information from clinicians, essentially identical between AI users and non‑users (Rock Health 2025 Consumer Adoption Survey, n=8,000, fielded December 2025).
* Insurers do not. Across the political spectrum — 57% of Republicans, 79% of independents, 84% of Democrats — majorities call insurer delays and denials a "major problem," and only 20% had heard much of anything about the June 2025 AHIP prior‑authorization reform pledge (KFF). Bipartisan agreement at this level is rare and should be read as a structural fact, not a news cycle.
* Digital engagement is real but fragmented. 77% offered portal access, 65% used it, 34% are frequent users (6+ times/year, more than double the 15% pre‑pandemic rate), 79% message providers through it, 77% schedule through it, 51% proxy/caregiver access — but only 7% use anything to unify multiple portals (ASTP/ONC).
* AI adoption is fast and institutionally unaligned. 32% of adults used AI chatbots for health information in the past year (KFF, Feb–Mar 2026); Rock Health finds the same 32%, doubled from 16%, with 64% of those users engaging weekly, 45% of Gen Z and 48% of Millennials. Critically: only 5% used a provider‑offered chatbot and 4% a payer‑offered one. They are using general‑purpose models. Nearly a third of AI users bring insurance questions to them. West Health/Gallup (n≈5,500, Oct–Dec 2025) finds one in four adults have used AI for health information, with trust split into near‑equal thirds and only 4% strongly trusting accuracy (West Health). Pew (n=5,111, October 2025) finds users rate these sources convenient and easy to understand far more than accurate (Pew). Uninsured adults are more likely than insured adults to use chatbots for health information even after controlling for age and income.
The synthesis: patients will share data with, and take advice from, whoever reduces their friction — and they are currently doing that with entities that have no fiduciary duty, no HIPAA obligation, no audit trail, and no accountability for being wrong. The incumbents' trust advantage is being spent down in real time by their own friction.

Forecast (my inference — not evidence)
2031. The dominant behavioral change is not that patients start shopping. It is that patients delegate the fight. An agent that reads the EOB, detects the discrepancy, drafts the appeal, and files the external review costs the patient near zero effort — and the base rates say it wins (80.7% of MA appeals overturned; ~48–73% of external reviews favorable). If appeal rates move from ~1% (marketplace) and 11.5% (MA) toward even 20–30%, insurer denial economics invert: a denial stops being free. I expect payer counter‑moves before I expect regulatory accommodation — appeal‑volume throttling, documentation demands designed to defeat automation, and terms‑of‑service restrictions on third‑party submission. Expect litigation over whether an AI agent may act as an authorized representative in an ERISA appeal.
Second, the cash tier institutionalizes. GLP‑1 direct pricing normalized the idea that the manufacturer or provider posts a number and you pay it. The structural poison pill is that these payments don't touch the deductible — which quietly creates a two‑track patient: insured for catastrophe, cash for everything routine. ICHRA accelerates this by converting the employer from plan sponsor to funder.
Third, portals lose to aggregators. 59% of people have multiple portals and 7% aggregate them today; the model context/agent layer solves this without anyone's permission.
2036. Two divergent paths, and I do not think the evidence picks between them.
* Path A — bifurcation. A cash/membership/direct tier with real price predictability serves people with discretionary income and simple needs. Insurance becomes genuinely catastrophic. The chronically ill and low‑income remain in the legacy adjudication system, which gets worse because the healthy and simple cases left. Everything in the ICHRA, DPC, medspa, and GLP‑1 data points this way.
* Path B — agent‑forced standardization. Machine‑readable prices become machine‑actionable, agents transact against posted rates, and the AEOB finally lands because the alternative is agents constructing their own estimates from 835 data and being right often enough to be embarrassing. Providers and payers standardize defensively.
My honest read: Path A is the default and Path B requires a forcing event. The forcing event most likely available is a binding‑estimate mandate with a remedy, which nobody is currently proposing.
Wildcards I will not forecast: whether enhanced subsidies return; whether the credit‑reporting question reaches an appellate court; whether CMS‑0057 APIs are extended to ERISA plans.
9. Rational-but-currently-impossible patient behavior
This is the product surface. Each item is something a rational actor would obviously do and structurally cannot.
1. Get a binding price. Not an estimate — a quote with a remedy for breach, covering every entity in the encounter. Impossible because no party can bind the others and the accumulator is nobody's authoritative record.
2. Appeal by default. Given an 80.7% overturn rate, the rational policy is "appeal everything appealable." Impossible because appeal is manual, per‑payer, per‑format, deadline‑laden, and requires knowing you have the right.
3. Carry an authorization across a plan change. Rational because the clinical facts didn't change. Impossible because authorization is a property of the payer relationship, not the patient.
4. Pay cash when cash is cheaper, and have it count. Cash beats the negotiated rate roughly half the time, but paying cash forfeits deductible credit — so the patient is penalized for choosing the lower price. This is a pure, unambiguous design defect.
5. Know the true, all‑in price of an episode rather than a service. Patients experience episodes; the system prices line items across multiple tax IDs.
6. Receive financial assistance without applying. Presumptive eligibility is explicitly permitted by 26 C.F.R. §1.501(r)‑6(c)(2) (e‑CFR). Hospitals mostly don't do it; ~$2.7B of self‑reported bad debt came from likely‑eligible patients.
7. See one longitudinal ledger of everything they owe and have paid, across all providers and plans, over time. No such object exists anywhere in US health care. The credit bureau is the closest thing, and it only tracks failure.
8. Delegate the whole administrative burden to a fiduciary agent with legal authority to act — receive the EOB, contest, negotiate, apply assistance, escalate. Impossible because there is no standing, no data access right that covers ERISA plans, and no authorization construct designed for a non‑human representative.
9. Comparison‑shop without doing work — i.e., have the shopping done for them at the moment of referral. All price tools require the patient to initiate; the behavioral evidence says 1–2% will.
10. Know, before choosing a plan, how often it denies. Denial rates range 1% to 54% across marketplace issuers and are published — but not at the point of plan selection, and not for employer plans at all, because that ACA transparency requirement was never implemented for group plans (KFF).

ASSIGNMENT 2 — THE PROVIDER / INSTITUTION LENS
1. The distinct institutional actors and their conflicting incentives
Format per actor: how paid · risk carried · admin burden · who bills · what they'd pay to fix.
Solo / small practice (≤5 physicians). Fee‑for‑service on the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule and commercial derivatives thereof; no negotiating leverage, often taking a payer's standard offer. Risk: cash‑flow risk, full owner liability, no reserve. Burden: highest per‑physician of any actor — 40 prior auths/week and 13 hours/week of physician+staff time (AMA 2025 survey). Billing: outsourced RCM vendor at 4–8% of collections, or one long‑tenured biller who is a single point of failure. Would pay for: getting paid faster and being told before the visit whether it will be paid. Note the trajectory: only 120,900 US physicians remain in physician‑owned practice, down 152,200 since 2018 (PAI‑Avalere 2018–2026).
Multi‑site independent group (10–200 physicians). FFS with modest contract leverage, plus ancillaries (imaging, lab, infusion, pathology) that carry the actual margin. Risk: operational and contract risk; some downside risk contracts. Burden: real, but amortized — this is the smallest scale at which a dedicated payer‑contracting and credentialing function is affordable. Billing: in‑house team plus vendor for A/R follow‑up. Would pay for: contract yield assurance (am I being paid my contracted rate?) and denial prevention. MGMA data shows independent physician‑owned practices improved their 0–30 day A/R share in 2024 while hospital/IDS‑owned groups' 120+ day share rose 9.5 to 15.9 points (MGMA Stat, July 2026) — independence is currently outperforming on collections.
PE‑backed platform (MSO + friendly PC). Management fee to the MSO; professional revenue to the PC. Risk: leverage. Debt service is the binding constraint and it converts clinical throughput into a covenant. Burden: centralized, and centralization is the thesis. Billing: captive RCM, often a platform‑level acquisition. Would pay for: anything that raises EBITDA before exit, and anything that makes multi‑entity roll‑ups reportable as one P&L. Context: physician medical groups were 46% of Q1 2026 US health‑services deal volume, up from 37% in Q1 2025, with deal count +18% YoY (PwC 2026 midyear outlook) — even as physician groups fell from 28% to 23% of global provider deal volume 2021→2025 (Bain 2026 Global Healthcare PE Report). Corporate entities now own 33.2% of US practices, exceeding hospitals' 30.6%, yet added only 4,200 physicians in 2024–2025 (+0.9%) while acquiring 8,000 practices — they are buying practices faster than they are retaining physicians, which is a signal worth sitting with.
Ambulatory surgery center. Facility fee under the ASC payment system, professional fee billed separately by the surgeon; frequently physician‑owned in whole or part, so the surgeon is both clinician and equity holder. Risk: case‑mix and implant cost risk; capacity utilization. Burden: moderate, concentrated in authorization and implant/device documentation. Billing: specialized ASC billing, in‑house or vendor. Would pay for: authorization certainty before the case is scheduled, because a cancelled block is unrecoverable. Structural tailwind: CY2026 OPPS finalized a three‑year phase‑out of the inpatient‑only list (285 procedures removed in 2026) and added ~547 procedures to the ASC covered procedures list (AHA, Holland & Knight). About 65% of ~6,468 freestanding ASCs remain independently owned (VMG Health 2026 M&A Report).
Hospital / health system. DRG inpatient, OPPS outpatient, facility fees, 340B spread, plus employed‑physician professional fees that usually run at a book loss subsidized by downstream facility revenue. Risk: the largest fixed‑cost base in the sector; payer‑mix risk; bad debt. Burden: enormous but industrialized — a hospital's RCM function is a factory. Billing: in‑house plus multiple vendors. Would pay for: denial prevention and point‑of‑service cash. The numbers: $48.4 billion in net revenue leakage in 2025 across 2,300 hospitals, up 25% from $38.6B in 2024 (Kodiak Solutions). Under direct attack in 2026 via site‑neutral payment: drug administration in excepted off‑campus provider‑based departments now pays at ~40% of the OPPS rate, a $290M cut.
Academic medical center. All of the above plus GME funding, research indirects, and a tertiary/quaternary case mix that makes medical‑necessity and length‑of‑stay denials existentially expensive. Risk: cross‑subsidy fragility — clinical margin funds the academic mission. Burden: highest complexity; multiple faculty practice plans with separate TINs. Would pay for: defensible clinical documentation at scale.
Plastic surgery practice. Hybrid, and the hybridity is the operational problem: reconstructive work is insured (with authorization and medical‑necessity fights over the exact same CPT codes that appear in cosmetic cases), cosmetic work is cash, and a single patient can be both in one encounter. Risk: reputational, cash‑collection, and the compliance risk of mischaracterizing cosmetic as reconstructive. Billing: small in‑house team that must run two entirely different financial models. Would pay for: clean separation of the two revenue streams with a shared clinical record.
Medspa. Nearly pure cash: AmSpa reports the US industry above $17B growing more than $1B/year, 10,488 locations in 2023, average revenue $1,398,833, 81% single‑location (Medical Spa Locator compilation of AmSpa data); market‑research estimates for 2026 range $9.3B to $21B depending on definition — treat the range, not any point estimate, as the finding. Risk: demand risk, device capital, and supervision/scope‑of‑practice regulatory risk, which is the real one. Billing: point of sale, memberships, packages, third‑party financing (CareCredit). Would pay for: patient acquisition and lifetime value, not claims processing. This actor has no claims problem and therefore shares almost no software requirements with the hospital — a fact naive "healthcare platform" models routinely get wrong.
Pharmacy. Reimbursed on ingredient cost plus dispensing fee, net of PBM adjudication, DIR/effective‑rate fees clawed back after the fact. Risk: negative‑margin dispensing on specific NDCs it cannot refuse to stock. Burden: real‑time adjudication (fast) plus prior authorization and appeals (slow). Would pay for: predictable net reimbursement. Now facing manufacturer‑direct channels (LillyDirect, NovoCare) that route around retail entirely.
Independent lab / imaging. High volume, low unit price, price‑taking under Medicare fee schedules with commercial rates benchmarked to them. Risk: volume and referral‑source concentration; a single health system in‑sourcing its own testing can end the business. Burden: order/authorization completeness and medical‑necessity documentation supplied by someone else — they bear the denial and don't control the input. Would pay for: clean orders with authorization attached. This actor's structural weakness is that its revenue depends on data quality produced by a party with no financial stake in producing it.
Billing company / RCM vendor. Percentage of collections (typically 4–9%), sometimes per‑claim or FTE‑based. Risk: their incentive is aligned to collections, not to margin — they profit from gross collections, so they are indifferent to the client's cost of the encounter. Burden: they are the burden, monetized. Would pay for: automation that lets them hold price while cutting labor. This is why AI adoption in RCM is fastest here: 63% of healthcare organizations report AI automation already integrated into revenue cycle workflows and 80% are exploring, piloting, or implementing generative AI for RCM, a 38‑point increase in under two years (Oliver Wyman 2026 RCM survey, n=200+ decision‑makers).
Management services organization (MSO). Management fee, typically a percentage of practice collections or a cost‑plus. Risk: regulatory — see Oregon, §5 and §7 below. Burden: it absorbs the practice's burden as its product. Would pay for: multi‑entity consolidation that survives a corporate‑practice‑of‑medicine audit.
IPA. Membership dues plus a share of contract value; may hold capitation and distribute it. Risk: if it takes global cap, it is an insurer without an insurer's reserves. Burden: network management, credentialing delegation, contract administration. Would pay for: attribution accuracy and cost/utilization visibility across independent members who do not share an EHR.
ACO. Shared savings and, increasingly, shared losses against a benchmark. Risk: benchmark risk, which is not clinical risk — you can improve care and still lose money because your region's spending fell. Burden: quality reporting, attribution reconciliation, data ingestion from claims feeds. MSSP PY2024: 480 ACOs, $2.4–2.48B net savings, over $4B in performance payments, 75% of ACOs earning shared savings, and 67% now in downside risk, with Level E/Enhanced ACOs generating $5.4B of the $6.6B gross savings (Health Affairs, Accountable for Health). Would pay for: knowing today what the benchmark will say in eighteen months.
The core conflict inside this list: the hospital wants volume in the highest‑paying site of service; the ASC and the independent group want that same volume in the lowest‑cost site where they own the facility; the ACO wants the volume not to happen; the RCM vendor wants the claim to be large; the MSO wants the EBITDA; the medspa wants none of it. These are not different customers for the same product. They are counterparties.

3. Where the money actually leaks, ranked
Ranked by dollars at risk for a typical provider organization, with the causal mechanism named:
1. Clinical denials — prior authorization and medical necessity. Kodiak attributes "virtually all" of the 2024→2025 increase in denial rates to precertification/prior‑auth and medical‑necessity denials. This is #1 because it is growing, it is concentrated in the highest‑paying commercial and Medicare Advantage business, and each instance consumes clinical staff time, not just billing time.
2. Patient responsibility that is never collected. Patient share rose to 7.3% of net revenue while collection yield fell to 42.4%. Providers are being handed a larger share of revenue in the form they are worst at collecting — and this is a direct consequence of benefit design decisions made by employers and payers, not by providers.
3. Front‑end data defects that surface as back‑end denials. Eligibility, coverage as of date of service, coordination of benefits, demographic errors. MGMA leaders name denials and appeals the largest leakage source (48%) versus front‑end issues (23%) — but that is a location attribution, not a causal one; a large fraction of denials originate in front‑end data.
4. Credentialing and enrollment gaps. Services rendered by a provider not yet enrolled with the payer are generally not retroactively billable. This is 100% loss, not delayed revenue. See §5.
5. Underpayment against contracted rates. Silent, systematic, and rarely detected without contract‑modeling capability. Small practices essentially never detect it.
6. Charge capture and documentation shortfalls. Services delivered and never billed; documentation that cannot support the level billed.
7. Site‑of‑service and payment‑policy shifts. Structural rather than operational: the CY2026 site‑neutral extension to off‑campus drug administration (~$290M), the three‑year IPO list phase‑out, and the CY2027 proposed PFS conversion‑factor reductions of 1.19% (APM QP) and 1.68% (non‑QP) as the one‑year 2.5% statutory bump expires (CMS).
8. Timely‑filing and appeal‑deadline expiry. Pure administrative forfeiture.
9. Infrastructure failure. Low probability, catastrophic magnitude — see the Change Healthcare discussion in §8.
Note the ranking's implication: the top two causes are both transfers imposed by other parties, not internal inefficiency. A provider organization can be operationally excellent and still leak, which is precisely why "we'll fix your revenue cycle" pitches meet skepticism.
4. Clinician experience, and how financial context intrudes on clinical decisions
Burnout is improving and still enormous. 41.9% of physicians reported at least one symptom of burnout in 2025, down from 43.2% in 2024 and 48.2% in 2023 — three consecutive years of decline, from nearly 19,000 responses across 106 health systems in 38 states (AMA Organizational Biopsy). The distribution matters more than the mean: emergency medicine 49.8%, urology 49.5%, hematology/oncology 49.3% at the top; infectious diseases 23.3%, ophthalmology 25.8%, pathology 28.3% at the bottom (Today's Clinical Lab summary). The AMA attributes the spread to workload, administrative burden, clinical environment, and staffing support.
Prior authorization is the named mechanism. 94% of physicians say prior authorization contributes to burnout; 95% say it delays necessary care; 92% say it negatively affects clinical outcomes; 79% report patients abandoning treatment because of it; more than one in four report prior authorization leading to a serious adverse event — hospitalization, permanent impairment, or death (AMA 2025, fielded December 2025, n=1,000; CMA summary). Burden is not uniform by payer: 75% rate UnitedHealthcare's burden high or extremely high, then Humana 65%, Anthem/Elevance 61%, Aetna 61%, Cigna 59%, BCBS 56%.
How financial context enters the exam room. Six concrete channels, each of which is a clinical decision being made on non‑clinical grounds:
* Anticipatory substitution. The clinician orders the drug or test they believe will be approved rather than the one they believe is best, to avoid a two‑week delay. This never appears in any denial statistic because no denial ever occurs.
* Documentation shaped for adjudication. The note is written to survive medical‑necessity review. Ambient AI documentation is accelerating this: vendors and analysts openly describe ambient tools as "helping providers bill at higher codes" by capturing more of the encounter (William Blair). The clinical record is becoming a billing artifact with a clinical side effect.
* Site‑of‑service steering. Where the procedure happens is increasingly determined by payment differential rather than acuity — and CMS is now actively legislating against the differential.
* Scheduling by authorization status. The ASC block goes to the case that is already approved.
* Ordering constrained by referral economics. Ancillary ownership shapes referral patterns; ACO attribution shapes them the other way.
* The abandonment problem. 79% of physicians report patients abandoning treatment over authorization barriers. The clinician bears the moral weight of a decision made by someone who never saw the patient.
The trend line to watch: 60% of physicians express concern that AI will further increase denial rates (AMA 2025), at the same moment providers are deploying AI to generate appeals. Both sides are automating. The volume goes up; the signal does not.
5. Credentialing, enrollment, and contracting — the chronic wound
Why it is chronic. Credentialing (verifying qualifications for privileges or network participation) and payer enrollment (establishing the billing relationship) are separate processes, run by different bodies, on different timelines, with no shared authoritative source. Every payer re‑verifies the same primary sources. Every hospital re‑privileges the same physician. Nothing is transitive.
The timeline. 90–120 days typical, 180+ for some payers, states, and specialties; over 85% of applications reportedly contain errors or omissions that extend the clock (Sutherland). Medallion's 2026 report finds 46% of hospitals need more than 10 days just to move a provider from intake to committee review (Hospitalogy, May 2026).
The financial mechanism, and why it's worse than it sounds. Payers generally do not permit retroactive billing before the credentialing effective date. This is not delayed revenue; it is destroyed revenue, against a salary that is being paid the whole time. Assured, benchmarked against BLS salary data, puts a physician's loss at up to $122,144 over a 120‑day delay (TechTarget). Sixty‑nine percent of health systems report losing $1,000–$5,000 per provider per day; one in five hospitals that can quantify it reports over $1M annually (Hospitalogy/Medallion). Caveat: the entire quantified literature here is produced by credentialing vendors, and the daily figures circulating in 2026 — $7,500/day, $10,122/day — come from vendor marketing (qualigenix, Veracity) and should be treated as upper bounds with a commercial incentive attached. The 90–120 day timeline itself is corroborated across independent sources and is the reliable core.
What happens when a provider joins or leaves mid‑stream — and what is portable. This is the precision question, so here is the precise answer, grounded in the CMS enrollment mechanics.
Attached to the individual clinician (portable):
* State medical license (state‑bound, but the clinician's).
* DEA registration (location‑linked, but the clinician's).
* Type 1 NPI — permanent, individual, never reissued, follows the person for life.
* Board certification, education, training, work history, malpractice claims history.
* The individual's Medicare enrollment record via CMS‑855I (PECOS).
* CAQH ProView profile.
Attached to the organization (NOT portable):
* Type 2 NPI, which is issued to the organization and is tied to the legal business name and TIN used to obtain it (CMS‑855B instructions).
* The Employer Identification Number / TIN itself.
* The payer contract and its negotiated rates. These belong to the entity, categorically.
* Every reassignment of benefits. A clinician authorizes an organization to bill and be paid for their services; CMS has now merged the former CMS‑855R into the CMS‑855I, and adding a reassignment requires signatures from both the individual and the group's authorized/delegated official, while terminating requires only one (CMS consolidated 855I bulletin, CMS‑855I). Both parties must be enrolled before the reassignment takes effect.
* Hospital privileges (facility‑specific).
* Facility accreditations, CLIA certificates, site enrollments.
* The patient panel, as a practical and often contractual matter.
Therefore, when a physician moves: the license, DEA, Type 1 NPI, and verified credentials move; the ability to be paid does not. The old group terminates the reassignment (one signature). The new group must accept a new reassignment and separately enroll the clinician with every commercial payer at the new TIN and every service location. The clinician can legally practice on day one and cannot generate collectible revenue for 90–180 days. Revalidation applications must list all active reassignments, so a clinician working at multiple TINs carries a lattice of relationships that must each be maintained.
The design lesson: any data model that treats "provider" as a single entity with attributes will be wrong at exactly this seam. The correct model has at minimum: person, license, individual identifier, organization, tax identity, location, and a time‑bounded, payer‑specific reassignment/participation relationship connecting them. Most systems collapse these, and the collapse surfaces as an unbillable claim.
6. Liability and accountability
Who actually bears the loss, per failure mode:
Failure	Who bears it
Wrong code	The billing entity whose TIN and NPI appear on the claim — legally, regardless of who selected the code. The physician attests to the documentation. If the coder is a vendor, the vendor's contract may indemnify commercially but does not transfer False Claims Act exposure.
Wrong estimate to a patient	Commercially, the provider — it becomes a write‑off or a fight. Legally, largely unallocated, because estimates are not binding. GFEs for uninsured patients carry a patient‑provider dispute resolution process; there is no equivalent for insured patients because the AEOB was never implemented.
Missed prior authorization	Almost always the provider, as a write‑off. This is the crux: the provider absorbs the loss for failing to obtain permission from a party that controls the process, the criteria, and the clock. Patients are typically protected from balance billing for a provider's authorization failure by contract.
False Claims Act exposure	The entity that submitted or caused submission of the claim, plus individuals under Yates‑style theories, plus acquirers via successor liability. FY2025 was the largest year in FCA history: $6.8B total, $5.7B health care, 1,297 qui tam suits (record), 401 government‑initiated investigations (DOJ). DOJ named three focus areas: managed care (Medicare Advantage risk‑adjustment coding), prescription drugs, and medically unnecessary care — with settlements including Independent Health up to $98M and Seoul Medical Group over $60M for unsupported diagnosis codes (DOJ fact sheet).
Failed medical‑necessity defense	The provider writes it off after appeal — the 2.7% final denial rate. The clinician who ordered it bears no direct financial consequence but bears the clinical one.
Employed clinician's individual exposure	Malpractice usually covered by the employer; billing/coding compliance exposure is not symmetric. A physician who attests to documentation used to support a fraudulent claim retains personal risk even when the organization built the workflow.
Two asymmetries worth naming plainly. First, the provider bears essentially all of the payer's process risk: missed authorization, insufficient documentation, and eligibility error all land on the provider's P&L, while the payer's cost of an incorrect denial is the 20% of appeals that get filed and overturned. Second, coding liability is one‑directional: undercoding costs the provider revenue quietly, overcoding costs treble damages plus per‑claim penalties loudly. That asymmetry is the strongest force shaping provider behavior in this entire section, and it is now colliding head‑on with ambient AI documentation tools explicitly marketed on their ability to support higher‑level codes.
7. Multi‑entity reality, and why it breaks naive software models
Here is the actual structure of a mid‑sized practice, and every layer is independently variable:
* Legal entity — a PC, PLLC, LP, or LLC. State‑specific. In CPOM states, the professional entity must be physician‑owned.
* TIN/EIN — the tax identity. A single brand may operate many. A single legal entity may hold one TIN across many locations.
* Type 2 (organizational) NPI — obtained using the exact legal business name and TIN. CMS explicitly contemplates "subparts": components of an organization that furnish health care but are not themselves legal entities, and which the organization must decide whether to enumerate with their own NPIs (CMS‑855B). Subparts are the mechanism by which the NPI hierarchy stops mirroring the corporate hierarchy.
* Type 1 (individual) NPI — the clinician, permanently, independent of employment.
* Service facility location — where care was rendered. Distinct from the billing address and distinct from the pay‑to address. Drives place‑of‑service coding, geographic payment adjustment, and — post‑CY2026 — whether the site‑neutral 40% reduction applies.
* Pay‑to entity — where the money goes. Set by the reassignment, not by who rendered the service.
* Rendering provider — CMS‑1500 Item 24J, the individual who performed the service (CMS MLN).
* Supervising provider — Item 17b when services are furnished incident‑to and the ordering person is not supervising. Rendering ≠ ordering ≠ supervising ≠ attending ≠ referring, and a single claim can carry four different NPIs in four different roles.
* Billing provider — Item 33, the entity submitting.
* Brand — has no representation in any of the above and frequently spans several TINs.
* MSO — manages, is paid a fee, and in CPOM states must be legally separated from clinical control (Oregon SB 951/HB 3410: MSO shareholders, directors, officers and employees may not collectively own or control a majority of the professional medical entity; MSOs may not make clinical decisions; most noncompetes, NDAs and nondisparagement agreements with licensees are void — effective January 1, 2026 for new arrangements and January 1, 2029 for pre‑existing ones (Holland & Knight, May 2026, Kirkland)). Other states including California have enacted CPOM/MSO restrictions; specific statutes and effective dates outside Oregon are NOT VERIFIED in this pass.
Why naive models break. Five specific failure modes:
1. Modeling "provider" as one object. As §5 shows, person, identifier, organization, tax identity, location, and payer participation are six things with independent lifecycles.
2. Assuming one location = one entity. A clinic can host three TINs; a TIN can span forty locations.
3. Assuming the corporate tree is the NPI tree. Subparts, joint ventures, and provider‑based departments break the isomorphism deliberately.
4. Treating the payer relationship as an attribute of the organization. It is a time‑bounded triple — (clinician, organization/TIN, payer/product) — with its own effective and termination dates, and every one of those dates is a billability boundary. A claim for a date of service one day before an effective date is not late; it is void.
5. Treating the brand as the entity. Patients, marketing, and consumer software all key on the brand. Money, liability, and compliance key on the TIN. They are never the same object and mapping between them is many‑to‑many.
The Oregon law adds a sixth: legal separation is now a first‑class modeling requirement, not an accounting fiction. A system that lets an MSO user approve a clinical decision may be evidence in a CPOM enforcement action.
8. What providers actually buy, and why they switch or refuse to
What they buy, in rough order of willingness to pay:
1. Cash, faster and more of it. Every purchase is ultimately underwritten by this.
2. Labor avoided in a specific, countable role. Not "efficiency" — headcount, at a named function.
3. Compliance defensibility. Insurance against §6.
4. Clinician time returned — the fastest‑growing category, because ambient documentation delivered a benefit clinicians could feel within a week. This is why ambient documentation is the most widely adopted AI application in the revenue cycle (Oliver Wyman, William Blair citing Bain October 2025 survey of 230 health system executives).
5. Patient acquisition — dominant for cash‑pay actors (medspa, aesthetics, DPC), near‑irrelevant for a capacity‑constrained health system.
The real evaluation criterion, which is rarely the one in the RFP: "Can I verify this working, at an organization like mine, and can I survive the switch?" Northwell's chief revenue officer describes working only with companies whose technology the system can see already in operation and whose users it can talk to, or where there is a co‑development structure with sufficient equity stake (HFMA). That is the criterion, stated plainly by a buyer.
Why they refuse to switch:
* Switching cost is measured in cash flow, not dollars. A botched RCM conversion means weeks of unbilled claims. The downside is asymmetric and immediate; the upside is gradual and contested.
* The EHR is the gravitational center. Anything that fights it loses. The credible disintermediation threat runs the other way: EHR vendors maturing equivalent RCM capability and competing for that market share (Oliver Wyman).
* The incumbent vendor holds the payer connections. Waystar alone claims more than one million providers connected via 100,000+ live integrations (HIMSS 2026 recap — vendor claims, unverified). Connectivity is the moat, not features.
* Data gravity and contract lock. Historical A/R, contract models, and denial history do not migrate cleanly.
* Concentration risk is now understood but not remediated. Change Healthcare processed roughly one‑third of US claims traffic. An AHA survey of nearly 1,000 hospitals in March 2024 found 74% reported direct patient‑care impact, 94% financial impact, and 33% said the attack disrupted more than half of their revenue; Kodiak measured a $6.3 billion drop in submitted claim value across its clients in the first three weeks (AHA); the breach ultimately affected ~190 million individuals (Healthcare IT News). Two years on, the structural concentration has not meaningfully changed, and smaller practices in particular have not funded secondary clearinghouse redundancy (Harris Secure Connect). Providers now understand the risk and still won't pay to fix it — which tells you the real discount rate on tail risk in this sector.
9. Provider trajectory, 2031 and 2036
Evidence (observed)
* Consolidation is near‑complete and still moving. 82.0% of physicians employed by hospitals or corporate entities as of January 1, 2026 (59.7% hospital, 22.3% corporate); 63.9% of practices non‑physician‑owned; 253,000 physicians shifted to employment since 2018; only 120,900 remain independent (PAI‑Avalere). Corporate practice ownership (33.2%) has passed hospital ownership (30.6%).
* But corporate employment growth has stalled while corporate acquisition accelerates. Corporations acquired 8,000 practices in 2024–2025 (+10.2%) while adding only 4,200 physicians (+0.9%). Hospitals acquired 5,800 and added 44,000 (+8.2%). Something is leaking out of corporate platforms.
* Distress is broadening. 20 healthcare bankruptcies covered in 2025 vs 15 in 2024; clinic and physician‑practice filings hit a six‑year high of 10 in 2024 against a 2019–2023 average of four; Chapter 11 filings rose 33% in Q1 2026 over Q4 2025; Steward's 2024 filing involved 31 hospitals, the largest health care bankruptcy in three decades (Becker's, June 2026, citing Gibbins Advisors).
* Regulation of ownership arrived. Oregon SB 951/HB 3410 with a 2026/2029 phase‑in; states enacting material‑transaction notification laws; FTC/DOJ sustained scrutiny of roll‑ups (VMG Health).
* Value‑based care is real, slow, and top‑heavy. MSSP PY2024: $2.4–2.48B net savings, eighth consecutive year of savings, 67% of ACOs in downside risk, with Level E/Enhanced generating $5.4B of $6.6B gross savings. CMS's stated goal remains 100% of traditional Medicare beneficiaries in an accountable care relationship by 2030 (CMS).
* Medicare physician payment continues to erode in real terms. CY2027 proposed conversion factors of $33.1693 (−1.19%) and $32.8409 (−1.68%) as the one‑year 2.5% bump lapses, against CMS's own projection of 2.5% growth in practice costs (AMA summary).
* AI in RCM crossed from pilot to production. 63% integrated, 80% exploring/piloting/implementing genAI (+38 points in under two years); the demand stack is ambient documentation, CDI, coding automation, and electronic prior authorization (Oliver Wyman). Autonomous coding is moving to "zero‑touch" for routine outpatient, radiology, and ED encounters; vendors now ship autonomous appeals agents (TechTarget). Practitioners caution that genuine end‑to‑end autonomy is a 3–5 year emergence and possibly a decade to maturity.
* Cash‑pay is a growing, structurally separate sector. Medspa: $17B+ and growing $1B/year, ~10,488 locations in 2023, average revenue ~$1.4M, 81% single‑location. DPC: ~3,136 practices, one added per day.
Forecast (my inference)
2031. Employment share plateaus somewhere in the mid‑to‑high 80s, but the composition shifts back toward hospitals and away from PE platforms, because the corporate cohort's physician‑retention problem (+0.9% headcount on +10.2% practice acquisition) is a leading indicator of platform stress, and CPOM statutes with 2029 compliance deadlines force restructuring precisely when many 2019–2022 vintage funds need exits. Expect a visible wave of distressed PE‑backed physician platforms between now and 2029, not after.
Autonomous coding becomes standard for routine outpatient encounters; the human coder role converts to exception management and audit defense. The under‑discussed consequence: when both coding and denial‑appeal generation are automated on the provider side, and denial generation is automated on the payer side, the adjudication process becomes machine‑to‑machine adversarial at a volume no human reviews. The IDR data already shows what that looks like — 4.8 million disputes against a 17,000/year projection. Regulators are not staffed for it.
Value‑based risk keeps growing inside Medicare and keeps failing to cross into commercial at scale, because commercial risk requires actuarial capability that provider organizations below roughly 500 physicians cannot build.
2036. The provider landscape most plausibly resolves into four distinct financial species that share almost no operational requirements:
1. Integrated systems operating at scale on thin margins with heavy public payer mix.
2. Specialty platforms — ASC‑centric, procedure‑dense, either physician‑owned or in their third institutional owner.
3. Risk‑bearing primary care — a genuinely different business, closer to insurance than to practice.
4. Cash‑pay direct care — DPC, aesthetics, and whatever the GLP‑1 direct channel matures into, with no claims infrastructure at all.
The forecast I hold with the most conviction, because three independent trends point at it: the boundary that matters in 2036 is not employed‑versus‑independent, it is claims‑bearing versus cash. Direct‑to‑employer contracting and ICHRA both erode the group plan as the unit of purchasing; manufacturer‑direct channels erode the pharmacy claim; DPC and aesthetics erode the professional claim. Each individually is small. Collectively they describe a system where the claim is no longer the universal transaction.
10. Where patient and provider interests genuinely conflict
Not softened. For each: the conflict, the mechanism, and who currently wins.
1. The patient wants a binding price. The provider cannot give one and does not want the liability of one. A binding quote transfers scope risk, adjudication risk, and coding risk from the payer and the patient onto the provider. The AHA's own 2026 position is that the current framework "is not delivering actionable information" and asks for AEOB implementation via a "mock claim" — i.e., a solution that routes the obligation through the payer. Providers support transparency in the abstract and have opposed the specific mechanisms that would bind them. Who wins: the provider. Five years post‑NSA, the insured patient still has no enforceable estimate.
2. The patient wants the lowest price. The provider needs the highest contracted rate, and cash is sometimes cheaper than insurance. Cash prices fall below the median commercial negotiated rate in roughly 47–49% of cases (Health Affairs, JAMA Netw Open). An insured patient in the deductible phase would frequently be better off paying cash — and would then forfeit deductible credit, which is the plan's design and the provider's protection. Nobody has an incentive to tell the patient this. Who wins: the provider and the payer jointly, at the patient's expense.
3. The patient wants low out‑of‑pocket cost. The provider now depends on collecting from the patient. Patient responsibility rose to 7.3% of hospital net revenue while collection yield fell to 42.4% (Kodiak). The provider's rational response — point‑of‑service collection, upfront deposits, payment plans, credit screening — is precisely the behavior that produces deferred care and medical debt. Top‑quartile organizations collect 28.4% at point of service versus a 16.4% median: the best‑performing revenue cycle is, from the patient's side, the most aggressive one. Who wins: currently the patient, in the narrow sense that 57.6% of what they owe goes uncollected — but they "win" by carrying debt, damaged credit, and, at ~20% of hospitals, denial of future non‑emergency care.
4. The patient wants care now. The provider wants the authorization first. The patient experiences the authorization delay as the provider's delay. The provider is protecting itself from a write‑off, because a missed authorization lands on the provider's P&L, not the payer's. Who wins: the payer. It has externalized the cost of its own utilization‑management program onto both counterparties and made them fight each other about it.
5. The patient wants to appeal. The provider's appeal is not the patient's appeal. Providers appeal for the contracted amount; patients appeal for coverage. These usually align and sometimes don't — most sharply when the provider would rather bill the patient as self‑pay than fight a denial worth less than the cost of appealing. Commercial outpatient net revenue leakage (10.3%) exceeds inpatient (6.6%) in part because low‑dollar outpatient denials aren't worth appealing (Enjoin analysis of Kodiak data). Who wins: neither. The payer wins by default, and the patient absorbs the residual balance.
6. The patient benefits from site‑neutral payment. The hospital is fighting it. CMS's CY2026 site‑neutral extension explicitly returns $70 million of the $290 million cut to beneficiaries as reduced coinsurance (Vizient summary). The AHA opposes it. That is a direct, documented, dollar‑denominated conflict between hospital revenue and patient out‑of‑pocket cost, litigated in the Federal Register. Who currently wins: split — CMS finalized the policy over hospital objection, but declined to extend site‑neutrality to on‑campus clinic visits, which is where the real money is.
7. The patient qualifies for charity care. The provider's incentives to find them are weak. Presumptive eligibility is permitted; screening before billing is required in only about 13 states; a 2015 study found only 44% of hospitals notified patients of assistance before attempting collection; and hospitals themselves reported ~$2.7B of bad debt from likely‑eligible patients (KFF, CFPB). A patient who is screened is a patient who becomes a write‑off instead of a receivable. Who wins: the provider, and the AMA's own House of Delegates has formally asked that this be changed (AMA Council on Medical Service report) — which is a professional body voting against its members' institutional employers' financial interest.
8. The patient benefits from medical debt leaving credit reports. Providers' collections leverage depends on it staying. Credit reporting is the enforcement mechanism behind voluntary payment. Remove it and self‑pay collection rates fall. The CFPB rule that would have removed ~$49B for ~15M people was vacated in July 2025 on a consent motion the Bureau itself joined. Who won: creditors and, indirectly, providers.
9. The provider needs accurate, complete coding. The patient bears the consequences of coding that is optimized. Ambient AI is marketed on its ability to capture more of the encounter and support higher‑level codes. Higher codes mean higher patient coinsurance. Simultaneously, FCA enforcement hit a record $5.7B in health care in FY2025, concentrated on MA risk‑adjustment coding. The patient is on the wrong side of both outcomes: they pay more when coding is optimized, and they pay through premiums and taxes when it is fraudulent. Who wins: the provider on the upside, the government on the downside, the patient never.
10. The provider wants the patient's clinical and financial data to stay inside the organization. The patient wants it to follow them. Portability is a competitive threat to a provider whose economics depend on downstream capture. Information blocking rules exist precisely because the commercial incentive runs the other way. Who currently wins: the provider, though the margin is narrowing — 65% portal access, CMS‑0057 payer‑to‑payer APIs in January 2027, and 51% proxy access are all eroding it.
11. Both would benefit from less administration. Neither can unilaterally stop. $496B in billing and insurance‑related costs, roughly half of it excess. Every actor's spending on this is defensive and rational given every other actor's. This is a genuine collective‑action failure rather than a villain, and the party that wins is whoever's friction is someone else's cost — which today is unambiguously the payer, because the payer sets the rules for a process whose labor cost is borne by providers and whose time cost is borne by patients.
The through-line. In nine of these eleven conflicts, the party who currently wins is the one that controls a process whose costs land on someone else's balance sheet. Patients and providers are frequently placed in direct opposition — over the bill, the estimate, the wait, the collection call — by rules that neither of them wrote and that transfer risk to whichever of them is less able to refuse. That is the conflict worth designing against, and it is not resolvable by making either party's existing workflow more efficient.
