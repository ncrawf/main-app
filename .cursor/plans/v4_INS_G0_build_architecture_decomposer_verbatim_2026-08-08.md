Includes health plan coverage, employer sponsorship, government program enrollment, HSA/FSA/HRA availability, financing arrangements, membership entitlement references, injury liens and workers' comp, research sponsorship, charity-care determinations, and self-pay.
* Must NOT own: what the plan covers; cost-sharing math; network status; money; the accumulator; the clinical need.
* Authoritative source: external for payer/government/employer/custodian/lender kinds; internal for membership, charity policy, and self-pay.
* Cannot merge because: it is the only place that can hold contradictory simultaneous beliefs about who pays, with per-belief provenance, and can represent "unknown" as distinct from "none." Merging it into benefit interpretation makes verification state invisible; merging it into commerce makes coverage a property of a transaction rather than of a person across time and organizations.
A2. Cost-Share Accumulator Reports [small] — the most debatable owner in my list.
* Owns: payer-reported consumption against deductibles, out-of-pocket maxima, visit/benefit/lifetime limits, with plan-year boundaries, the payer's own internal as-of date, and our reconciliation against claims we know are in flight.
* Must NOT own: a single authoritative "remaining deductible" number. The output is always a range with a confidence and an as-of.
* Authoritative source: payer, absolutely and irrecoverably. Our copy is structurally partial because other providers' claims move it.
* Cannot merge because: it changes on a different clock than coverage (claim-driven vs. enrollment-driven), resets on plan-year boundaries, is retroactively restated, and is the single largest error term in every estimate. Merge argument I take seriously: it arrives on the same wire response as A1, so splitting creates a join on the hot path. I would merge it into A1 only if measurement shows the two never need independent refresh cadences — which I doubt, because you re-pull accumulators far more often than you re-pull enrollment.
A3. Coverage Determination Grants
* Owns: prior authorizations, pre-certifications, referrals, medical-necessity determinations, step-therapy and formulary exceptions. Each carries a scope (person, rendering provider, facility, code set, units, date window), a units-authorized/consumed/remaining ledger, an expiry, and a revocation path.
* Must NOT own: the clinical decision to treat; the payment obligation (an approved auth is explicitly not a guarantee of payment, and every payer says so); the claim.
* Authoritative source: payer/PBM/utilization-management vendor.
* Cannot merge because: it is prospective and creates a weak forward commitment with a consumable balance; adjudication is retrospective and creates money. They have different consumers, different timing relative to care, and different relationships to the clinical seam — auth touches the care-decision boundary and adjudication does not.
A4. Adjudication Determinations
* Owns: every counterparty statement about the disposition of our submissions: acknowledgments and front-end rejections, line- and claim-level allowed/paid/adjusted/patient-responsibility splits, reason and remark codes, reversals and takebacks, offsets applied against unrelated claims, post-payment audit findings, and independent-dispute-resolution outcomes.
* Must NOT own: money movement (a remit can precede, accompany, or follow the money, or arrive with no money at all, or be net-negative); the patient's balance; our opinion of whether they were right.
* Authoritative source: payer, and occasionally a third party (clearinghouse acknowledgment, IDR entity).
* Cannot merge with claims because: determinations arrive unsolicited, arrive for claims we did not submit (same TIN, different entity), restate prior determinations years later, and are the one thing we may never edit. Fusing them with our claim record produces the single worst bug class in revenue systems: an internal process "fixing" a payer's number.
Zone B — Bilateral agreements
B1. Network Participation & Contracted Economics
* Owns: participation as a five-axis tuple — contracting entity (TIN), individual provider (NPI), service location, payer, product/line-of-business — with effective periods; the negotiated rate methodology (fee schedule, percent-of-Medicare, case rate, per diem, capitation, value-based bonus terms); and the provider-enrollment/linkage state that determines whether a specific clinician may yet be billed under that contract.
* Must NOT own: the applied allowed amount on a specific claim (that is A4 — the payer's assertion, which frequently disagrees with the contract, which is precisely how you detect underpayment); the chargemaster; cash prices.
* Authoritative source: jointly authored. Our contract copy is ours; the payer's roster is theirs; they disagree constantly, and the disagreement has money and legal consequences under surprise-billing protections.
* Cannot merge because: is_in_network: boolean is the canonical failure. This owner must hold "we assert participating, they assert not, contested since date D," and must not resolve that to a single truth. Note the internal sub-split: participation status is increasingly public under transparency rules, while rate terms usually carry contractual confidentiality — different access-control postures inside one owner, which is a fair argument for splitting it in two.
Zone C — Our assertions to counterparties
C1. Claim Submissions
* Owns: what we asserted to whom — billing entity, rendering and supervising providers, service facility, coded lines, units, charges, coverage referenced, auth referenced, submission channel, correlation identity, and version relationship (original / replacement / void).
* Must NOT own: the clinical fact, the code (that is D2, which it references by version), the disposition, the money, the patient's balance.
* Authoritative source: us — and frozen at transmission.
* Cannot merge because: one service occurrence produces many claims (professional, facility, anesthesia, pathology, interim bills, secondary and tertiary sequencing), and one claim can span many occurrences. There is no 1:1 with anything.
C2. Authorization & Information Requests [small]
* Owns: what we asked for, when, with what supporting evidence, through what channel, and the correlation to A3's answer.
* Must NOT own: the grant itself.
* Cannot merge with A3 because: an unanswered request is a fact with a deadline and a liability, and it must be representable when no grant exists. Also, statutory turnaround clocks run against the request, not the answer.
C3. Contested-Determination Submissions — explicitly plural and typed
* Owns: appeals (with level), open-negotiation notices and IDR initiations, audit rebuttals and records productions, underpayment recovery demands, and our responses to patient billing disputes.
* Must NOT own: the outcome (A4), and must not be one object. This is where the "universal case" trap lands. These five are different: a payer appeal has statutory levels and deadlines and produces a restated determination; an IDR is baseball arbitration with a binding result and a losing-party fee; a payer audit is counterparty-initiated with legal document-production duties; a patient billing dispute must suspend collections and has consumer-protection rules; an underpayment recovery is a contract claim against B1. Collapsing them yields a case table with fifteen nullable columns and five contradictory state machines fighting inside one status enum.
Zone D — Our derived interpretations
D1. Benefit Terms & Interpretation Rulesets
* Owns: the versioned, machine-executable representation of plan terms — covered/excluded services, cost-share design, tiering, medical-necessity criteria, auth requirements, visit limits — plus provenance to the source plan document and the effective period of each version.
* Must NOT own: whether this person is covered; the accumulator; the price; the estimate.
* Authoritative source: the plan document is external; our interpretation of it is internal and canonical-as-applied. This distinction is load-bearing: when the patient disputes, the question is not "what does the plan say" but "what ruleset version did we execute on March 3."
* Cannot merge because: rulesets are reusable across thousands of people and must be independently versioned, tested, and pinned.
D2. Billing Representation (Coding)
* Owns: the coded, payer-facing translation of documented clinical reality — diagnoses, procedures, modifiers, units, place of service, drug identifiers, revenue codes, grouper outputs — plus the coder or model that produced it, the review state, and the clinical-documentation query loop.
* Must NOT own: anything in clinical memory, in either direction. Coding has a strictly one-way read dependency on clinical truth and zero write capability into it.
* Authoritative source: us. Human-accountable even when machine-produced.
* Cannot merge because: codes are revisable without touching the clinical record (rebill with corrected codes, note unchanged), the liability posture is entirely different (upcoding is fraud; a note error is a clinical amendment), and code validity is date-of-service dependent, not submission-date dependent.
D3. Obligation Allocation — arguably an extension of commerce; see the test below.
* Owns: the append-only allocation of each charge across responsible parties — payer-responsible, patient-responsible (split by deductible/coinsurance/copay/non-covered), contractual write-off, sponsor-responsible, financing-assumed, charity, and explicitly unallocated — with the inputs and policy version that produced each allocation, and reversal links.
* Must NOT own: balances or money state (commerce keeps those), or the reason codes in raw form.
* Cannot merge because: it depends on payer-domain inputs commerce must not know about, and must be reproducible independently of current balance state, because retroactive coverage changes force reallocation years later. The test for whether to merge: if commerce's obligation ledger is genuinely a multi-party, append-only, reversible allocation ledger already, merge it in. If commerce's ledger assumes buyer = patient = payer, do not merge — fix commerce first.
Zone E — Our communicative acts
E1. Financial Disclosure Statements
* Owns: the immutable record of financial statements made to a person — pre-service estimates, good-faith estimates, advance explanations of benefits, surprise-billing notice-and-consent, financial-assistance determinations, and statements of amount due — each pinned to the exact input versions that produced it.
* Must NOT own: any obligation. An estimate creates no debt.
* Cannot merge because: the content is derived, but the fact that we said this, to this person, at this time, is canonical, immutable, and legally operative. This is the sharpest instance of a general principle: a projection that is communicated becomes a first-class truth about a speech act. If estimates are recomputed on read, you have destroyed evidence.
Zone F — Shared capability (not a domain)
F1. Counterparty Exchange Envelope — correlation identity, channel and credential selection, idempotency, retry and backoff, timeout semantics, per-transaction cost accounting, raw-artifact retention, parse provenance, and replay. This is deliberately not a domain: it carries no business semantics. Every subject-matter owner keeps its own state machine. The envelope is what makes twelve owners cost roughly four mechanics.
Amendments to owners you already have
* Identity gains counterparty organizations and their transaction-specific routing identities (payer IDs differ by clearinghouse and by transaction type — a genuine, ugly, real problem), plus payer-asserted person identity projections, because the payer's name/DOB/sex-marker record will disagree with yours and claims reject on the mismatch.
* Consent gains financial and disclosure consent kinds — assignment of benefits, financial responsibility, disclosure to payer for payment purposes, disclosure to sponsor, disclosure to lender or collections — and one specific right that most designs miss entirely: a patient who pays in full out of pocket may demand the service not be disclosed to their plan. That is a hard veto on claim assembly, and in a mixed medspa/clinical episode it is not hypothetical.
* Documents gains card images, plan documents, explanation-of-benefit artifacts, authorization letters, appeal packets, and records productions — with a disclosure log that must be retrievable a decade later.
* Commerce keeps money state, payments, refunds, retail and cash pricing, memberships, and the obligation ledger — but must give up three assumptions: that the buyer is the patient, that a refund returns to the person who paid the card, and that entitlement is created by payment. Under insurance, the patient often owes after care. The entitlement trigger must generalize from "paid" to "financially cleared under policy," where clearance may be payment, an accepted estimate, an authorization, a sponsor guarantee, or nothing at all in an emergency.

§2. Lifecycles
L1. Funding participation record. asserted (unverified) → verification-attempted → verified-as-of-T → stale → superseded → retroactively-terminated or retroactively-added → archived. Never deleted. Correction: new assertion with supersession link; the old one stays because decisions were made on it. Timeout: no answer produces an explicit unknown, never not covered. Authority: payer for the fact; us for the record of belief. The killer transition: retroactive termination back-dated 90 days, which invalidates estimates, authorizations, and posted payments simultaneously.
L2. Verification exchange. needed → queued → submitted → channel-accepted → answered / channel-rejected / no-response-timeout / unparseable → superseded-by-newer. Timeout is a terminal state with meaning, not an error to swallow. Some payers charge per transaction and some silently return cached data, so idempotency and cost accounting are not optional.
L3. Benefit interpretation version. drafted → validated → active-for-period → superseded → retired. Correction: never edit active; supersede. Anything that consumed a version pins it forever.
L4. Network participation. contracted → effective → amended → terminated (with notice / immediate) → retro-corrected, with an orthogonal contested state when our position and the payer's diverge. No terminal state; periods reopen.
L5. Authorization. not-required / requirement-unknown / required → drafted → submitted → acknowledged → pended-for-information → peer-review-scheduled → approved / partially-approved / denied → appealed → active → partially-consumed → exhausted → expired → extended → retro-revoked / voided. Timeout semantics are jurisdictional: in some states, failure to respond within the statutory window is deemed approval. That rule must be configuration, not code. Authority: payer for the grant; us for the request; a clinician for the peer-to-peer, which is a clinical act.
L6. Financial disclosure. computed → issued (immutable) → superseded-by-revised → invalidated-by-material-change → reconciled-against-actual. Correction: issue a new one; keep the old. A variance beyond a configured threshold triggers a re-disclosure obligation.
L7. Coded charge. captured → coded-draft → reviewed → query-pending → finalized → held (edit failure, missing auth, missing coverage, provider not yet enrolled) → released → claimed → recoded-for-rebill → written-off-uncoded. Authority: coder or model under named human accountability; clinician answers queries; billing staff never touch documentation.
L8. Claim. assembled → scrubbed → held → submitted → clearinghouse-accepted or rejected → payer-acknowledged → in-adjudication → adjudicated → paid / denied → replaced or voided → secondary-billed → patient-billed → closed → reopened. Terminal states are provisional by design, because audits and retroactive coverage changes reopen closed claims years later. Timeout: no acknowledgment within N days is "lost," a distinct state from "denied," and timely-filing clocks are running.
L9. Adjudication determination. received → parsed → matched / orphan (very common) → posted → restated → reversed → offset-applied. Authority: payer only. We dispute; we never edit.
L10. Money. Existing commerce lifecycle, plus: payer electronic payment, virtual-card payment (which carries a fee — model it), patient payment, refund to patient, refund to payer, offset (money that never moves but changes two balances), financing disbursement, pre-tax-account authorization and settlement and substantiation-failure reversal, sponsor invoicing, and write-off classes that are contractually and legally distinguishable.
L11. Contested determinations — five separate machines. Payer appeal: eligible-until-deadline → level-1 → level-2 → external review → exhausted. Independent dispute resolution: open-negotiation window → initiation → entity selection → offer exchange → binding determination. Patient billing dispute: raised → collections suspended → investigated → adjusted / upheld / partial. Payer audit: records-requested → produced → findings → rebuttal → demand → paid / offset / appealed. Underpayment recovery: variance-detected → demanded → resolved. Different deadlines, different evidence, different outcomes, different regulators. Do not unify.
L12. Financing application. offered → applied → decisioned (approved with terms / counter-offer / declined) → accepted → funded → serviced-by-lender. The trap is the refund path: if a patient financed the episode and later gets a refund, the money usually must go back through the lender, not to the patient.
L13. Pre-tax account use. expense-eligibility-determined → card-authorized → settled → substantiation-requested → substantiated / unsubstantiated → reversed or repaid → plan-year-forfeited. The eligibility-of-expense question is a different question from eligibility-of-person, owned by tax rules, and is exactly where a mixed clinical/cosmetic episode breaks.
L14. Regulatory obligation instance. triggered → due-at-T → satisfied / breached → remediated. Generic, but it must be first-class rather than buried in billing code, because breaches have penalties and need an owner.

§3. Seams
For each: what crosses, direction, authority, and what must not.
1. Clinical memory → Coding. Read-only, one-way, by identity and version. Never crosses back: codes are not clinical facts. Documentation-improvement queries cross the other way but must be non-leading, attributable, and answered as a clinical act.
2. Coding → Claim. Coded lines plus provenance. Must not cross: narrative clinical content, except as an explicitly governed attachment under minimum-necessary scoping.
3. Consent → Claim assembly. A restriction crosses as a veto. Must not cross the other way: no billing need may relax a consent restriction, and no "helpful" automatic coordination-of-benefits may route a restricted service to a plan.
4. Funding participation → Estimate and Claim. Coverage identity, hierarchy, and as-of. Subtle rule: a paid claim is strong evidence of coverage, but it enters as a new assertion with provenance "inferred from adjudication," never as a retroactive verification.
5. Adjudication → Allocation → Commerce. Allocation events cross. Must not cross: raw reason codes into a patient-facing balance; patients get meaning, not CARC/RARC.
6. Estimate → Patient. An immutable statement. Must not cross: any obligation. An estimate is not a charge.
7. Authorization → Care decision. Auth status crosses to scheduling and estimation as advisory context. Must not cross: the ability to cancel a clinical order. An auth denial may trigger a human decision; it may never be a system-enforced gate on care.
8. Network determination → Estimate and patient notice. Crosses as an input and as a trigger for surprise-billing disclosure. Must not: silently change an already-quoted price.
9. Sponsor (employer) seam. Eligibility rosters and invoices cross inbound and outbound. Identified clinical detail never crosses to a sponsor, and the consent that would permit it is suspect by construction in an employment relationship — make it hard, make it logged, make it individually scoped.
10. Lender seam. Amount, identity, and at most a service category. Never diagnoses.
11. Payer records seam (for auth, appeal, audit). A governed disclosure with minimum-necessary scoping, logged with what was sent, to whom, under what authority, retrievable for a decade.
12. Cross-organization seam — the one the multi-org requirement forces. Coverage participation and payer-reported accumulators are properties of the person; network status, contracted rates, claims, and allocations are properties of the operating organization. Org A's claims must never be visible to Org B, yet Org B's claims move the accumulator that determines Org A's estimate accuracy. Resolve by sharing the payer's accumulator assertion (the payer is the common source) and never the other organization's claim data.
13. Exchange envelope seam. Correlation, provenance, and channel metadata cross freely; business semantics never live in the envelope.

§4. External vs. internal
Genuinely external — projected, provenanced, never edited by us: coverage existence and terms and periods and member identifiers; accumulator state; plan documents; the payer's network roster; prior authorization grants; adjudication results and remittance detail; payment instruments; formulary and pharmacy pricing; pre-tax account balances and expense-eligibility rules; financing decisions, terms, and servicing balances; employer eligibility rosters; government program enrollment; published government fee schedules; all code sets (and note CPT is licensed, which constrains redistribution in your own APIs); national provider identifiers and credentialing status; clearinghouse acknowledgments; arbitration determinations.
Genuinely internal: what care was decided and delivered; what we documented; what we coded; what we charged; what we claimed; what we told the patient; what we collected and refunded; how we allocated; our policies; our own network position; our consent records.
Bilateral, which is neither: the contract itself, and network participation. A design that forces these into one column will pick a side and be wrong half the time.
The five that get botched, specifically:
1. Network status modeled as an internal boolean when it is a contested bilateral position.
2. Patient responsibility — the same words name two different epistemic objects. Before adjudication it is our estimate (internal opinion, no obligation). After adjudication it is the payer's determination (external assertion we have a contractual duty to honor). Giving them one field is the origin of most patient billing complaints.
3. Allowed amount — the contract says X, the remit says Y. Y is external truth about this claim; X is agreement truth. Overwriting X with Y destroys your only underpayment detector.
4. Accumulators treated as our number.
5. "Active" eligibility treated as durable. A verification response says active as of the moment they answered, according to data that may itself be days stale on their side. It is not a promise about the date of service, and it can be retroactively falsified.
On timestamps: internal facts need two axes (effective, recorded). External assertions need five: effective in the world, asserted-by-them, received-by-us, recorded-by-us, and known-to-the-decision-maker. And there is a sixth hiding inside the fifth — the counterparty's own internal as-of, which they sometimes disclose and usually do not. If your model has one created_at, none of the disputes in §7 are answerable.

§5. Read-models and projections
Every one of these must carry an as-of and a confidence, and none may ever be a write target.
* Intake readiness — what is known, what is stale, what is missing, what to collect at the desk, with confidence. Not "is the patient covered."
* Pre-service patient view — expected cost, why, the cash alternative, and what happens if the plan says no.
* Post-service patient statement — unified across funding sources and across clinics within one organization, in plain language, reconciled against the estimate ("we told you $X, you owe $Y, here is the delta and its cause"), with a visible dispute affordance. An explanation of benefits is not a bill; the projection must teach that distinction rather than blur it.
* Clinician view — deliberately narrow: authorization status and material affordability signals. Genuine tension here between informing the clinician and biasing the clinician; I would show cost as context to a human and forbid it as an input to automated clinical recommendation.
* Coder worklist and AR/denial worklists — cohorted by cause, not by age alone.
* Contract performance — paid versus contracted variance, which is the join of B1 against A4 and the only way to detect systematic underpayment.
* Payer scorecards — denial rates, turnaround, appeal overturn rates, silence rates. This becomes strategically important when agents negotiate.
* Compliance reconstruction — "what did we know, when, from whom, and what version of what rule did we run." This is the projection that makes the whole design defensible in an audit or a lawsuit.
* Sponsor view — aggregate only.
* Patient's agent view — machine-readable, signed, verifiable, with explicit provenance and confidence. This is a 2030 requirement, not a nice-to-have.
* Cross-organization longitudinal financial view — assemblable only with explicit patient consent and only from person-scoped facts.

§6. Invariants
Clinical/financial firewall — the highest-value group:
1. No financial state may be a precondition for recording a clinical fact.
2. Coverage, authorization, and denial never write to clinical memory. They may be referenced by a clinical decision; they are not clinical assertions.
3. A payer's denial of payment is not a determination that care was unnecessary, and no projection may render it as one. This matters more each year as AI summarizers flatten nuance.
4. Coding never writes to clinical memory. Documentation queries are non-leading, attributable, and answered by a clinician.
5. Care must be deliverable with all financial state unknown. Non-blocking by construction, not by a policy toggle that someone can flip.
6. Financial data may be displayed to a clinician but must never be an input to an automated clinical recommendation without explicit disclosure and governance.
7. Research-sponsored and certain trial-related services must be routable away from insurance billing — a clinical-context routing rule that is not about money at all.
Epistemic integrity:
8. Unknown is representable and distinguishable from false, zero, and none. Unverified coverage is not "no coverage"; no response is not a denial.
9. No internal actor may write to an external-owned fact class, ever. Corrections to external facts are disputes, not edits.
10. External assertions are append-only; a restatement is a new assertion with a supersession link.
11. Every external assertion carries source, method, channel, and all five timestamps.
12. Every automated artifact — card OCR, AI coding, AI-drafted appeal, AI-assembled auth packet — is labeled with model, version, inputs, and a named accountable human. An AI assertion must never be indistinguishable from a human or counterparty assertion.
13. Any estimate, allocation, or claim is reproducible from pinned input versions.
Financial integrity:
14. Estimates create no obligations.
15. Obligations arise only from a delivered service with a coded charge, or an explicit contract (membership, deposit, package). Never from a projection.
16. Allocations are append-only; reallocation is a reversal plus a new allocation. Balances are derived and never directly set.
17. Allocations for a charge sum to the charge at every instant, with "unallocated" as an explicit bucket.
18. Money movement and obligation allocation are independent facts that may occur in either order.
19. A transmitted claim is immutable; changes are new versions with typed relationships.
20. A voided or reversed claim reverses its allocations rather than deleting them.
21. Refunds route to the party that paid, in proportion, respecting financing and pre-tax-account rules.
22. Credit balances owed to payers are liabilities with statutory return deadlines, not float.
23. Retroactive change never silently moves money; it produces an explicit, reviewable reconciliation event.
24. Discounts and write-offs are validated against program rules — routine waiver of cost-sharing for government-program beneficiaries is not lawful, so a membership discount cannot be applied blindly.
Process and rights:
25. Deadlines are computed from authoritative timestamps, and a missed deadline is a recorded event with accountability, never a silent write-off.
26. A patient dispute suspends collections and adverse reporting.
27. A consent restriction is a hard veto on claim assembly and any payer disclosure.
28. Minimum necessary on every outbound disclosure, with a retrievable log.
29. Sponsors never receive identified clinical data.
30. Cross-organization isolation of claims, rates, and allocations.
31. There is no universal case object. Each contested lifecycle is its own type.
32. Scheduling never creates charges. Only service occurrence, or an explicit prepaid contract, does.
33. Identity merges never orphan or silently re-attribute claims and obligations — remember the old identifier is already out the door on submitted claims.

§7. Hard problems and traps
Genuinely hard:
* Accumulator truth is unknowable in principle. Your estimate depends on a number owned by the payer and moved by providers you cannot see. The honest ceiling is a probabilistic estimate with a stated confidence and a promised reconciliation. Evidence that would sharpen this: decompose historical estimate-versus-actual variance into accumulator staleness, coding drift, network error, and interpretation error. Whichever dominates is where to invest; I would guess accumulators, but I would not build on that guess.
* Retroactivity everywhere. Retroactive termination, retroactive Medicaid eligibility, retroactive contract amendments, retro-denials, takebacks years later. Bi-temporality is necessary but not sufficient — you also need a replay engine that can recompute allocations without rewriting history, and an explicit policy for when not to chase small amounts.
* Mixed financing inside one episode. A visit containing a covered dermatologic service and a cosmetic service, partly funded by a pre-tax account that covers only the former, with a membership discount that a payer contract may prohibit, in a state where cosmetic services are sales-taxable, where the patient invoked non-disclosure on the cosmetic portion. Splitting that correctly — including allocation of genuinely shared costs like the facility fee and the single thirty-minute slot — is the hardest concrete problem in the prompt, and it is the one your medspa reality guarantees you will hit.
* Bundling and global periods. Charges and payables have a many-to-many, time-dependent relationship. A service inside a global surgical window is not separately billable; edits bundle line pairs; inpatient groupers absorb detail. "What did I receive" and "what is payable" are different lists, and the patient projection must reconcile them without lying in either direction.
* Orphan remits and offsets. Money with no matching claim, recoupments taken against unrelated claims, net-zero remittances, virtual cards with fees. This breaks ledgers built on the assumption that payments attach to claims.
* Counterparty non-determinism. The portal disagrees with the wire response, which disagrees with the phone call, which disagrees with tomorrow's answer. You must hold contradictory external assertions simultaneously, pick one for a decision, and record both the choice and the conflict.
* Underpayment detection requires re-implementing the payer's pricer. It will never be exact.
* Provider enrollment lag couples credentialing to billing: you cannot bill for a clinician not yet linked to the contract, and the workarounds (supervising provider, locum rules) are legally constrained.
* Cross-org coherence under confidentiality. The patient is one person; the organizations may be competitors.
* How binding is a number we said? Estimates are not contracts federally but support dispute rights, some states are stricter, and some contracts require honoring quotes. I do not think this is resolved anywhere, and I would model "bindingness" as an explicit attribute of a disclosure rather than assume it.
* Agent-versus-agent adversarial dynamics in 2030. When both sides automate, denial-optimizing and appeal-optimizing agents create an arms race with real patient harm in the middle. Automated denial at scale is already in litigation. The substrate should record enough to prove how our determinations were made and should demand the same of counterparties.
Traps a competent team walks into:
Building one "Insurance" domain. Building one case/task/workqueue object for auths, denials, appeals, audits, and patient disputes. is_active and is_in_network as booleans. Overwriting the eligibility record on each check. Computing the estimate on read so you can never reproduce what you told the patient. Mutating balances instead of appending allocations. Letting a coder or an AI coding loop close without a clinician. Making authorization a gate on scheduling or ordering. Assuming claim-to-encounter is 1:1. Assuming one patient, one coverage, one payer. Treating the explanation of benefits as the payment. Treating "denied" as terminal. Hard-coding reason-code handling. Building the current wire format's shape into the domain model — and equally, abstracting so far that you cannot round-trip required wire fields, so keep a stable domain core plus lossless retention of the raw artifact at the adapter edge. Building estimates from the chargemaster instead of contracted rates. Defaulting unknown coverage to self-pay and billing the patient. Treating financing as a payment method rather than a substitution of the obligated party. Treating the sponsor like a payer. Assuming the contracting entity, the employing entity, and the facility owner are the same organization. Letting a reconciliation script write to the balance projection.

§8. One area, several, or none?
Several — twelve small owners in five write-authority zones, sharing one exchange capability, plus four amendments to existing owners.
Why not none. Absorption fails on ownership grounds. Commerce cannot own coverage, because coverage is a property of a person over time across organizations, not of a transaction. Commerce cannot own adjudication, because commerce writes its own truth and adjudication is a counterparty's append-only assertion. Clinical memory cannot own coding, because coding must be revisable without touching the clinical record. Consent cannot own authorization, because an authorization is a counterparty grant with a consumable unit ledger. Every absorption candidate violates the single-owner principle you already hold.
Why not one. A single "Insurance" area would contain, in one place, facts owned by us, facts owned by them, facts jointly owned, derived interpretations, and immutable speech acts — with four incompatible correction semantics. It would inevitably grow a case object and a status enum that means five things. It would also be misnamed: the same machinery must serve employer direct contracting, injury liens, research sponsorship, financing, and non-US financing models. Naming the area after one 2026 US instrument is exactly the "big noun becomes a domain" error.
Why granular is affordable. Owner count is not the cost; protocol count is. Twelve owners across five zones share about four mechanics — append-only-with-provenance, immutable-on-transmission, versioned-and-reproducible, and append-only-allocation — plus one exchange envelope. That is a small conceptual surface.
The strongest argument against my own answer, stated as forcefully as I can:
Every one of my twelve owners is an instance of the same pattern: a bilateral relationship with an external organization, in which we make provenanced assertions, they make provenanced assertions, we derive interpretations from both, and money follows. If that is true, then twelve owners is not decomposition — it is enumeration of the instances of one abstraction, and I have mistaken a rich instance space for a rich type space. The practical consequence is severe: nearly every screen and every decision needs six of the twelve, so you will build a coordinating service to join them, that service will accumulate the logic, and within a year it will be the god-object I claimed to avoid — except now it is undeclared, sitting in an integration layer with no ownership discipline at all. A single "Payer Relations" area with rigorously enforced internal zones might preserve every invariant in §6 at a fraction of the coordination cost, and internal boundaries are cheaper to correct than architectural ones.
I do not think that argument wins, because write authority genuinely differs and it is the boundary that produces the worst bugs when violated. But I hold it seriously, and here is the evidence that would settle it: map the correction and reversal path of each of the twelve. If they collapse into one or two mechanisms in practice, the abstraction is real and I have over-split — merge. If they stay distinct (and I expect a retroactive coverage termination, a recoded rebill, a payer takeback, and a superseded estimate to be four genuinely different reversals), the split is earned. Second test: count how many of the twelve are ever written by the same actor in the same transaction. If the answer is "most, most of the time," I am wrong.

§9. What must be data-driven
Configuration — versioned, effective-dated, reviewable, and regression-tested against historical cases (a rule change must never silently alter a past determination):
Code sets and their validity windows; bundling and edit rules; modifier semantics. Per-payer, per-product, per-plan facts: authorization requirements, timely-filing windows, appeal levels and deadlines, submission channels and transaction-specific identifiers, format quirks, reason-code meanings and handling policy. Jurisdictional rules: deemed-approval on authorization timeout, prompt-pay windows, surprise-billing protections and their thresholds, state-specific consent forms, medical-debt reporting restrictions, sales taxability of cosmetic services. Regulatory triggers and thresholds: which disclosures are required when, the dispute-triggering variance amount, nonprofit financial-assistance applicability. Benefit rulesets themselves. Rate methodologies. Allocation policy: application order, rounding, unallocated handling, write-off thresholds. Financial assistance and discount policy, with program-eligibility guards. Automation confidence thresholds (auto-post versus human review). Retention schedules. Which disclosures require which consent.
Hard-coded — the physics, which should be genuinely difficult to change:
Multi-axis time and append-only history. Owner-of-truth boundaries and write authority. Allocation conservation. Immutability of transmitted claims and issued disclosures. Mandatory provenance on external assertions. Unknown as a first-class value. The clinical/financial firewall. Consent as a hard veto. Cross-organization isolation. Reproducibility from pinned inputs.
The rule is short: rules change, physics does not. And the configuration itself needs governance — it is not a settings page, it is a versioned artifact with a review gate, because a bad rate table is as damaging as a bad migration.

§10. What must be true in 2035
1. Agent-to-agent negotiation is the primary channel, and negotiations are multi-turn. A naive design models everything as one-shot request/response keyed to today's transaction types. That forecloses multi-turn negotiation, joint evidence construction, conditional commitments, and the moment at which something becomes binding. The exchange envelope must be able to record a negotiation transcript — offers, evidence, signatures, and a binding event — as first-class evidence.
2. Coverage arrives as a signed verifiable credential presented by the patient's agent, not as a card photo plus a round trip. My decomposition already permits this because assertion is separated from exchange: a credential is just another assertion with a different verification method. A design that models "eligibility check" as a procedure rather than "coverage assertion with provenance" forecloses it.
3. The person carries their financial and coverage history across organizations. Coverage participation must be person-scoped, portable, exportable, and consent-shareable from the start. Scoping coverage to the tenant is a structural foreclosure — arguably the hardest of all of these to undo later.
4. Determination happens at or before the point of care, not weeks after. Adjudication must not be modeled as "the thing that happens after a claim is mailed," or real-time and pre-service adjudication become a special case bolted on.
5. The substrate must be able to run the payer side. Capitation, direct-to-employer contracting, shared-savings, and full risk mean the operating organization sometimes is the entity that adjudicates, holds risk, attributes populations, and owes other providers. This is the single most important 2035 point. If you hard-code "we bill, they pay," you cannot represent the arrangement that the market is most clearly moving toward. My zone model survives this because zones are defined by write authority, not by which side of the seam you sit on — but only if you never name a field payer_paid_amount.
6. Non-US and multi-jurisdiction. Do not name owners "insurance," "claim," or "EOB." Name them funding participation, cost assertion, and determination.
7. Automated determinations are legally interrogable on both sides. Expect a right to human review and a duty to explain. Every automated determination needs a modeled escalation-to-human transition and a retained decision context — model version, inputs, policy version — for a decade or more. You should also expect to demand the counterparty's rationale and to store it.
8. Payment rails change. Instant settlement, programmable escrow, outcome-conditional release. Money movement must stay abstracted from obligation, which invariant 18 already gives you.
9. Consent is per-purpose, per-recipient, time-boxed, and revocable. A single global assignment-of-benefits checkbox will not survive.
10. Payer-to-payer and payer-to-provider data exchange mandates mean better sources will exist than yours. Your projections must be able to reconcile with, and defer to, a superior source — which requires that they were always labeled as projections. This is the payoff for being rigorous in §4.

Uncertainty register
Where I am genuinely unsure, and what would resolve it:
* Accumulators as a separate owner (A2). Resolve by measuring whether refresh cadence and reconciliation logic differ materially from coverage. If they do not, merge into A1.
* Allocation as a separate owner (D3) versus a commerce extension. Resolve by inspecting whether commerce's obligation ledger is already multi-party, append-only, and reversible. If it is, merge. If it assumes buyer equals patient, fix commerce rather than route around it.
* Network participation and contracted rates as one owner (B1) or two. The confidentiality-posture difference is a real argument for two. Resolve by looking at access-control requirements: if rate terms need materially stricter controls than participation status, split.
* Whether the whole thing is one abstraction over-split into twelve. Resolve with the two tests in §8 — the reversal-path map and the same-actor-same-transaction count.
* How binding a communicated estimate is. Genuinely unresolved in the industry. Model bindingness as an explicit, configurable attribute of each disclosure rather than assuming a default.
* How much financial context a clinician should see. I asserted an invariant (display allowed, automated influence forbidden) with more confidence than the evidence supports. This deserves an explicit governance decision rather than an architect's preference.
